Inner product and norm

An inner product measures alignment. The norm is a length. StatHarbor Analytics drew these twelve panels.

Twelve quick panels: name the object, not the proof. Ask who still uses cross products outside three dimensions. Pace at roughly one panel every twenty seconds if you show them all.

Matrix multiplication

\(\eta = X\beta\) is the linear predictor. Each column of \(X\) is a covariate. Each row is one observation.

Tie \(X\) to a design matrix they will see in GLMs. One line on dimensions: \(n\) rows, \(p\) columns.

Least squares

Least squares minimises \(\|y - X\beta\|^2\). The residual is orthogonal to the columns of \(X\). That orthogonality is the projection from the notation block.

Stress orthogonality as geometry, not algebra trivia. It returns on the geometry row of the statistics map.

The hat matrix

\(P_X\) projects \(y\) onto the column space. When \(X\) has full column rank, \(P_X = X(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\), and \(\hat y = P_X y\).

The hat matrix name is deliberate: \(\hat y\) is a linear function of \(y\). Mention rank only if someone asks about collinearity.

A quadratic form

\(x^{\mathsf T}Ax\) is a squared length that depends on \(A\). A variance, and a Mahalanobis distance, are this shape.

Ellipses on the panel are the picture to remember. Skip eigenvalues here; the next slide names them.

Spectral decomposition

A symmetric matrix rotates to principal axes and then scales by its eigenvalues. Principal components are this decomposition of a covariance.

One sentence on PCA if the room is mixed: rotation then stretch. Do not open a PCA example.

Singular values

A singular value decomposition does not need \(A\) to be square. It is the same geometry for a rectangular design matrix.

Rectangular \(X\) is the link to regression. SVD is optional detail unless someone works on high-dimensional data.

Positive semidefinite

A covariance is positive semidefinite: \(x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma x \ge 0\). Cholesky writes \(\Sigma = LL^{\mathsf T}\), which is how a simulation draws a correlated vector.

PSD is the guardrail for valid covariances. Cholesky is a one-line simulation trick, not a full lecture.

Score and information

The score is the gradient of the log-likelihood. Observed information is the curvature. More curvature means a more precise estimate.

Bridge to MLE: score zero at the optimum. Fisher information reappears on Wald asymptotics later in the deck.

A conditional covariance

The Schur complement is the covariance of one block after conditioning on the other. Conditioning on a covariate is this reduction.

Only for audiences comfortable with partitions. Otherwise say “partial out a block” and move on.

The identities the other panels use

Transpose, inverse, trace, and \(\partial \|y-X\beta\|^2 / \partial \beta = -2X^{\mathsf T}(y-X\beta)\) are the working rules behind the estimator and the score.

Reference slide: people photograph this one. Read the derivative line only if someone asks where the normal equations come from.

Mahalanobis distance

\(d_M(x,\mu)^2 = (x-\mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\) measures distance after scale and correlation. The level sets are ellipses. Outlier rules that ignore \(\Sigma\) treat those ellipses as circles.

Mahalanobis is the multivariate cousin of a \(z\)-score. Ellipses versus circles is the misuse to flag.

Two spreads, not one

\[ s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar Y)^2} \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar Y) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

\(s\) describes how far the observations sit from their mean. The standard error describes how far that mean would move if the sample were drawn again. Dividing by \(\sqrt{n}\) is the whole difference. In the draw on the next slides, the standard deviation of the 1,000 means is 2.11, against \(15/\sqrt{50} = 2.12\).

Read both formulas once. The common mistake is reporting \(s\) where the reader expects an uncertainty for \(\bar Y\). Ask the room which number would go in an error bar for the mean.

Multivariable is not multivariate

Timothy Achala drew the distinction this way. Click the figure if the type is small. The wording we will police is say the precise word.

One \(Y\) versus several correlated outcomes: let the diagram do the work. Ask who has seen “multivariate logistic” in a paper title when the authors meant several predictors.

One outcome, or several

Multivariable: one \(Y\), several predictors. A logistic regression of detection on age, sex, body mass, and smoking is multivariable. It is not multivariate.

Multivariate: several responses, in one model, with the correlation between them. A multivariate probit, or a GEE for several binary outcomes. Systolic and diastolic pressure together, not as two separate fits.

Calling the first model “multivariate logistic regression” names a different analysis. Clinical papers often swap the words. Reporting guidelines give the reason to keep the words apart:

  • ICH E9(R1)
  • CONSORT

Thirty seconds. The misuse is in the adjective, not the link function. Invite one example from the room before you move to the statistics map.

Expectation

What functional of the distribution summarises behaviour?

\[ \mathbb{E}_P[X], \quad \psi(P), \quad \mu \]

Statistics targets functionals of distributions, not raw data.

  • Means
  • Risks
  • ATE
  • Prediction error
  • Utility
  • Other targets \(\psi(P)\)

Variance and uncertainty

How variable is a quantity or estimator?

\[ \mathrm{Var}(X), \quad \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \]

Variance measures dispersion, not bias or distance to truth.

  • Standard errors
  • Confidence intervals
  • Hypothesis tests
  • Uncertainty quantification

Uncertainty is information about precision, not a problem to eliminate.

The standard deviation and the standard error are the two scales that sentence has to keep apart.

Likelihood and optimisation

Which parameter best fits the observed data under a model?

\[ \hat{\theta} = \arg\max_{\theta} \ell(\theta) \]

\[ S(\theta) = \nabla_{\theta} \ell(\theta) \]

  • MLE
  • GLM
  • Regularisation
  • Model selection (AIC, BIC)
  • Estimation procedures

‘Best’ is defined relative to the assumed model and criterion.

AIC and BIC change the criterion, not the meaning of likelihood. Ask what “best” would mean if the mean were misspecified.

Tangent space and local models

What infinitesimal variations of the model are possible?

\[ S(Z) = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial \epsilon} \log p_\epsilon(Z) \right|_{\epsilon = 0} \]

Scores span the tangent space.

  • Efficiency bounds
  • Semiparametric inference

Asymptotics

What happens as sample size grows?

\[ \hat{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu \]

\[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0, V) \]

  • CLT
  • Delta method
  • Wald inference
  • Bootstrap
  • Asymptotic theory

Large samples stabilise estimators but do not eliminate bias or misspecification.

Sampling effort is a covariate

If sample A has twice the reads of sample B, it has about twice the chance of showing any given taxon, with no biology involved.

Model

\[ \log \mu_i = \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \log(\text{library size}_i) \]

The last term is an offset: a column with a coefficient fixed at 1. The model is then about a rate per read, not a raw count. The fitted version is one mean, two variance assumptions.

This is the slide where a modelling idea comes from a property of the measurement rather than from a textbook. Note the offset is not a covariate whose coefficient is estimated. If someone asks about rarefying to a common depth instead: it throws away data and it does not remove the dependence, and the offset is the model-based alternative. Keep it short; the evidence is two slides later.

Why a Gaussian lm is the wrong first tool

  • A count cannot be negative, and a Gaussian mean can predict a negative count
  • The variance of these counts rises with the mean, so one \(\sigma^2\) does not fit
  • A proportion is bounded, and a rise in one taxon is a fall in another’s share
  • Zeros are a property of community tables, not rows to delete

The evidence for the second line is on the mean against variance slide. The deep dive is after the pitfalls.

What is measured here

The response in this dataset is a gene copy number per gram of dry peat, from quantitative PCR (Roth et al. 2024).

  • 12 plots \(\times\) 9 depth increments \(\times\) 2 sampling months in 2021: 216 rows
  • Bacterial, archaeal, and fungal copies, each with a log column
  • Missing numeric values are coded \(-9999\) in the published file

A qPCR copy number is an estimated abundance, not a counted integer. It is a positive, strongly skewed continuous response, so it belongs with the transformation discussion and not with the Poisson.

Be explicit that this is not a count, even though it looks like one. Treating a qPCR abundance as Poisson would be a category error, and it is a mistake the room will have seen. The sentinel value is the other teaching point: 15 values in this file are -9999, and a script that reads the column as a number without that rule will report a mean of about -9999 times the missing fraction. The rule lives in scripts/prepare-teaching-data.R and nowhere else.

The model the design is asking for

For plot \(j\), depth \(i\):

Model

\[ \log_{10} Y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \mathrm{depth}_{ij} + \beta_2 x_{1j} + \beta_3 x_{2j} + \beta_4 x_{1j} x_{2j} + u_j + \varepsilon_{ij} \]

Two different scores

Prediction, on new units:

\[\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2}\]

Explanation, for one coefficient, under the linear model:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}, \qquad t = \hat\beta_j / \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j)\]

A small RMSE does not say that \(\hat\beta_j\) is the effect of changing \(x_j\).

You do not need to derive the normal equations. The takeaway is two symbols on the slide: RMSE for prediction, \(t\) for one coefficient. If someone asks about causality, park it until the covariate slide.

A more flexible fit can predict worse

On their held-out rows, the linear model had RMSE 1.01 and the random forest 2.40.

The forest lost because the mean really was linear, and the noise covariates gave the trees something to chase. Complexity is not a better prediction. Interpretability is not a worse one either: the linear coefficients are the explanatory object, and here they also predicted better.

A hold-out score does not certify the coefficient

Cross-validation answers “how close is \(\hat{Y}\) on new rows?”. It does not say that \(\hat\beta\) is unbiased, or that the path from \(x\) to \(Y\) is the one you would intervene on.

Check the linear assumptions, and refit after removing influential rows, when the claim is about \(\beta\). Keep the hold-out for a prediction. Ranking, calibration, and the decision are three further checks.

Cross-validation is not a substitute for a causal estimand or for checking influence on \(\hat\beta\). If the room is quiet, ask: “Your AUC is 0.9 — can you still intervene on \(x_j\)?” That usually opens the right debate.

2 · An effect, an interval, and a reason it matters

The two intervals are the published example in Riley and colleagues (Riley et al. 2022).

Two intervals, two different failures. The narrow one is precise and small: detected, and possibly irrelevant. The wide one is large and uncertain: absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. The CA-SYS rate ratio later today is the first kind, and the tillage richness contrast is the second. Neither is read off a p-value.

Detection is not importance

Statistical evidence asks whether the data could have come from a world with no effect. Practical evidence is a threshold the investigators set in advance: a minimal important difference. It is not a formula, and \(p\) does not supply it (Olszewski).

Replace the clinical threshold with a flux, a stock, or a decision that would change. The estimate and the interval stay in the sentence (Halsey et al. 2015).

This slide used to be two, which said the same thing twice. The one idea is that significance and importance are separate judgements and only one of them comes from the data. Ask the room what change in soil carbon would alter a management decision. Most groups have never written that number down, which is the point. In a confirmatory analysis it is written before the fit.

A yes-or-no decision has two errors

A Type I error rejects the null when it is true; its long-run rate, when the test is calibrated, is \(\alpha\). A Type II error fails to reject the null when it is false; its rate is \(\beta\), and the power is \(1 - \beta\) (Biswas).

The table is a decision table, and it only makes sense once you have agreed to make a yes-or-no decision. That is a choice, not an obligation. The two rates are properties of the procedure across many replicates, not statements about the study in front of you. Keep that distinction, because the next slide needs it.

\(p\) is not that rate

If the treatment has no effect, a \(p\) of 0.03 means that 3% of replicate studies would obtain an effect this large or larger.

  • Comparing that number with \(\alpha\) is the yes-or-no decision
  • The number itself is a probability about the data, not about the hypothesis
  • \(\alpha\) is fixed before the data; \(p\) is computed from them

The careful statement is the replicate definition, and the rate of false assertions is \(\alpha\).

The commonest error in the room will be to read p as the probability that the null is true. Say the two conditional probabilities out loud: P(data this extreme given no effect) against P(no effect given the data). Only the first is available from a frequentist test, and only the second is what most readers want.

A p-value is about the data

Under identical replicates of this experiment, with the effect set to zero, \(p\) is the probability of a result more extreme than the one in hand (Harrell, ch. 22).

A \(p\) of 0.06 says that, under the model and the null, a replicate would be more extreme about 6 times in 100. It does not say that the null has probability 0.06.

Write “given \(H_0\)” on the board if you have one. The 0.06 example is deliberately awkward — neither side of 0.05 — to keep the focus on the conditional, not on the threshold ritual.

α is a rate of false assertions

\(\alpha\) is the long-run probability of asserting an effect when the effect is exactly zero. It is not the probability that a positive result is false, and it is not “the probability of an error”.

A procedure that assumes the null cannot report the probability that the null is true. Fisher’s 1956 wording treated a 1% test as a 1% rate of mistaken decisions, which swaps \(P(\text{assert} \mid H_0)\) with \(P(H_0 \mid \text{assert})\) (Harrell).

\(\alpha\) is chosen before data; \(p\) is computed after. The Fisher quote is the Bayesian confusion in one line — read it slowly.

The tail belongs to a model

Guillaume Rousselet takes a one-sample, one-sided \(t\) test: \(n = 50\), \(t = 1.52\), \(p = 0.0670\) when the imaginary experiments are normal, independent, of fixed size, and always analysed with that \(t\) test.

Sampling the same tail from the beta distribution the scores actually follow moves the simulated \(p\) from 0.0675 to 0.0804. Change the model and the number moves (garstats 2026).

This is the “same \(t\), different likelihood” slide. You are not asking them to abandon \(t\) tests; you are showing that \(p\) is computed under a story. If they only remember one line: the tail belongs to the model.

Surprise, on a bit scale

\(-\log_2(0.05) = 4.32\). A \(p\) of 0.05 is the surprise of four heads in a row (Greenland, 2019).

Read \(p\) as compatibility between the model and the data, from 0 to 1, not as a verdict (Greenland et al., 2016).

The coin analogy lands with non-statisticians. Follow with: compatibility is not “probability the null is true”. Keep it short — the Halsey exchange is next.

One P, or a screen of them

Halsey and colleagues simulated a small normal experiment and showed a single \(P\) jumping across 0.05 from sample to sample. They called that fickleness a source of irreproducible decisions (Halsey et al. 2015).

van Helden repeated the simulation. The interval crossed the null in the same runs where \(P > 0.05\), so swapping the interval for \(P\) did not change the decision. He put the instability on the small sample, not on an intrinsic property of \(P\) (van Helden 2016).

Halsey, Curran-Everett, and Drummond replied that the shared mathematics was not the recommendation. The recommendation was to read the effect and its margin of error, rather than to re-apply a threshold (Halsey et al. 2016).

Step all three fragments. The van Helden point matters for methodologists; for this room, fragment three is the punchline. Do not re-litigate NHST — point back to the effect slide.

A planned question is not a screen

Huber called the exchange a clash of cultures (Huber 2016).

  • A craftsman reports one \(P\) for one question written in advance.
  • An industrialist uses \(P\) to screen thousands of hypotheses; the histogram of all of them is part of the evidence.
  • One \(P\) can fall anywhere in \((0,1)\) when the null is true; the distribution of many \(P\) values is a different object.
  • Both sides agree that \(P\) alone is an insufficient report.

Genomics and high-throughput ecology are the industrialist case. Ask whether anyone has seen a “significant” result from a fishing expedition without multiplicity control. One minute.

Five readings to retire

7 · \(I^2\) is not the size of the heterogeneity

Quantity Read as
\(\tau^2\) Absolute spread of true site effects
\(I^2\) Share of scatter that is heterogeneity relative to sampling noise
Study-level regression on proportions Not the same as a within-site slope (ecological bias)

\(I^2\) is a relative share of variance. Small studies can show a small \(I^2\) while the between-site variance is large. Report that variance. A regression on site means is not the core-level slope. The arithmetic starts at one site, one estimate. Sampling noise shrinks with \(n\); a bias does not (random and systematic error).

This slide is the warning label; the next ten minutes are the arithmetic. If someone quotes \(I^2 = 25\%\) as “low heterogeneity”, ask for \(\tau^2\) in the effect’s units.

One site, one estimate

In this block \(i\) is a study or a site, and \(y_i\) is that site’s estimated effect. It is not the core-level response \(Y\).

\[ y_i = \theta_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, s_i^2) \]

  • \(y_i\): the estimate you observe
  • \(\theta_i\): the true effect at that site
  • \(s_i^2\): the sampling variance of the estimate

Two sites with the same \(\theta\) can still disagree, because of \(\varepsilon_i\).

Say the change of index once. Here i is the site, which is the cluster. The core-level response is still Y, and it is not on this slide. About one minute. The next two slides separate that sampling noise from a bias that more rows will not remove.

Random error shrinks

Thiese, Ronna, and Ott define random error as noise around the true value, above and below it. It is not a bias. On the previous slide it is \(\varepsilon_i\), with variance \(s_i^2\). A larger sample at that site makes \(s_i\) smaller, which is the same move as the standard error.

Selection, a misread instrument, and a confounder are not this noise. They shift every row the same way.

Connect \(\varepsilon_i\) to the standard error they already know. Random error is what replication within a site would average down; confounding is not that.

A larger sample does not shrink a bias

Their example is a scale that adds the same offset to every person. More people do not remove it. Figure 1 in that paper, adapted from Rothman, is this shape. The curves are illustrative, from scripts/generate-slide-figures.R.

The flat line is the punchline for observational carbon work too: more cores do not fix a wrong unit or a confounder. Pair with the missing-data MNAR message later if needed.

Fixed effect, or random effects

  • Fixed effect
  • Random effects

\[\theta_i = \mu, \qquad w_i^{\mathrm{FE}} = 1/s_i^2, \qquad \tau^2 = 0\]

The \(y_i\) differ only by sampling. The pooled mean weights each site by \(w_i^{\mathrm{FE}}\).

\[\theta_i = \mu + u_i, \quad u_i \sim N(0, \tau^2), \qquad y_i \sim N(\mu,\, s_i^2 + \tau^2)\]

The same two lines are a hierarchical model. In the design block: fixed and random name the target.

Arrow keys move between the tabs. Fixed effect: one theta. Random effects: the thetas are draws. Do not say the random-effects mean is automatically the causal effect. One minute on each tab. The design-block version is the slide “fixed and random name the target”.

\(\tau^2\) is how far the true effects spread

\[ \theta_i \sim N(\mu, \tau^2) \]

\(\tau^2\) is on the squared scale of the effect. It is not a percentage.

Arithmetic, not a carbon dataset: \(\tau^2 = 0.09\) on a log risk ratio gives \(\tau = 0.30\), so a rough band for the site effects is \(\mu \pm 1.96\tau \approx \mu \pm 0.59\). Call that large or small only after you name the scale.

A precise site with \(s_i^2 = 0.01\) has fixed-effect weight \(100\). With \(\tau^2 = 0.09\) the random-effects weight is \(1/(0.01+0.09) = 10\). That site no longer dominates the pool.

Stress the units. Then the weight: heterogeneity stops the most precise site from deciding the mean alone.

\(I^2\) compares that spread with the noise

If the sampling variances are similar,

\[ I^2 \approx \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + s^2}. \]

The usual estimator starts from Cochran’s \(Q\), the weighted sum of squared departures from the fixed-effect mean:

\[ Q = \sum_i \frac{1}{s_i^2}\,(y_i - \hat\mu_{\mathrm{FE}})^2, \qquad I^2 = \max\left(0,\, \frac{Q - (k-1)}{Q}\right). \]

Arithmetic: \(Q = 40\) and \(k = 11\) studies give \(I^2 = (40-10)/40 = 75\%\). \(Q\) asks whether the scatter exceeds sampling error. \(I^2\) turns the excess into a share of \(Q\). Neither replaces \(\tau^2\).

Write Q, then I-squared, on the board if someone treats 75 percent as a large tau. Under no heterogeneity, Q is about k minus 1.

The same \(\tau^2\) can produce either \(I^2\)

Keep \(\tau^2 = 0.04\).

  • Noisy sites, \(s^2 = 0.16\): \(I^2 = 0.04/0.20 = 20\%\)
  • Precise sites, \(s^2 = 0.0025\): \(I^2 = 0.04/0.0425 \approx 94\%\)

The true effects did not move. The measurements got sharper, so heterogeneity is a larger share of what you see.

Work the arithmetic aloud with a calculator if needed. Same \(\tau^2\), different \(I^2\) — that sentence is worth repeating.

The soils did not move

Discrimination is the ranking

Utility is whether the score changes the action

A product is not a sum

Match the transform to \(v(\mu)\)

The variance travels with the mean

In a Gaussian model the mean and the variance are separate parameters. After a log they are tied: a log-normal variance depends on the mean. A power chosen only to stabilise the spread changes that relationship even when the power is not a logarithm.

Ordinary least squares is BLUE

The Gauss–Markov theorem: if ((_i) = 0), the (_i) are uncorrelated, each has the same finite variance (^2), and () has full column rank, then

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y} \]

has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators. The errors do not have to be Gaussian for that result. Gauss used normality. Markov removed it.

The (F) test compares two nested models

The (F) statistic on the same printout compares the fitted model with the intercept-only model. The statistic is the drop in the residual sum of squares, per extra parameter, divided by the remaining residual mean square:

\[ \frac{\{\mathrm{SS}(\mathrm{mean}) - \mathrm{SS}(\mathrm{fit})\}/(p_{\mathrm{fit}} - p_{\mathrm{mean}})}{\mathrm{SS}(\mathrm{fit})/(n - p_{\mathrm{fit}})}. \]

Deviance is the residual sum of squares

Lack of fit is measured by the deviance. The null deviance \(D_0\) compares the intercept-only model with the saturated model. The residual deviance \(D\) compares the fitted model with the saturated model. Maximising the log-likelihood is the same operation as making \(D\) small.

For nested models the likelihood-ratio statistic is the drop in deviance,

\[ D_{\text{small}} - D_{\text{large}} \sim \chi^2, \]

with degrees of freedom equal to the number of extra parameters. In R that is anova(model1, model2, test = "Chisq"). This is the GLM form of the analysis of deviance.

Their pseudo-\(R^2\) is \(1 - D/D_0\). They say that several pseudo-\(R^2\) exist, and that a nested comparison should be the likelihood-ratio test. AIC is the comparison they allow when the models are not nested: deviance, plus a penalty for the number of parameters.

A gamma latent rate is a negative binomial

A point mass of zeros is a mixture

A zero-inflated model is a different generative story. With probability \(\pi_0\) the observation is a structural zero. With probability \(1-\pi_0\) it is a draw from a count distribution \(f\). StatHarbor writes \(\pi_0\) as \(p\).

\[ \Pr(Y=0) = \pi_0 + (1-\pi_0)\,f(0), \qquad \Pr(Y=k>0) = (1-\pi_0)\,f(k). \]

The fitting criterion is cross-entropy

The difference of the two weights equals the one-hot slope: \(\Delta\mathrm{WOE} = 2.9957 = \hat\beta_1\). Subtracting a constant does not change a variance, so the standard error of a weight of evidence equals the standard error of that category’s log-odds.

With a single categorical predictor the two codings describe one model. With several categorical predictors, Burakov notes that one-hot coefficients move when the reference level changes, while the weights of evidence stay centred on the overall event rate.

Softmax is the logit for \(K\) classes

Flexibility spends variance

A kernel is a local bump

Before the regression, the same idea estimates a density. Each observation contributes a bump of width \(h\):

\[ \hat f_h(x) = \frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n} K\!\left(\frac{x_i - x}{h}\right). \]

They name four kernels: Gaussian, Epanechnikov, uniform, and triangular. Bandwidth matters far more than which of those four you pick.

Local weights for \(\mu(x_0)\)

GCV chooses the bandwidth

A linear smoother is \(\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{Y}\). Leave-one-out cross-validation refits \(n\) times. Generalised cross-validation approximates it:

A global polynomial moves the whole curve

Extend the line by powers of \(x\). A quadratic uses \(x\) and \(x^2\). Stop at degree 3 or 4: higher powers become strange near the ends of \(x\). A global polynomial also means a local change can alter the fit far away.

A regression spline spends its flexibility at knots

Model

Knots \(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_K\) cut \(x\) into intervals. A cubic piece is fitted in each, with \(f\), \(f'\), and \(f''\) continuous at the knots. The truncated-power basis is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \sum_k \theta_k (x-\xi_k)_+^3\).

The analyst chooses how many knots, and where. A common default is the quantiles of \(x\). After the continuity constraints, each added knot costs about one degree of freedom, and the spline is most flexible where the knots are dense. A step function, a piecewise constant, misses the trend unless the cut-points are real breaks. That is the dichotomisation case.

In the model callout, read the truncated-power basis once, then say software rarely uses it raw. Knot placement is a scientific choice disguised as a default quantile rule. The dichotomisation link is deliberate: cutting \(x\) is a spline with too few pieces and wrong continuity.

B-splines span the same curves

The truncated-power basis is clear and numerically unstable. A B-spline basis spans the same spline space with local support: each basis function is zero outside a few intervals. The design matrix is sparse, and the fit is better conditioned. Software prefers this basis. The knots are still chosen by hand. The next smoother removes that choice and penalises roughness instead.

Students who implement splines meet instability before they meet the maths. Local support means a knot move affects only a band of basis functions—good for interpretation and for sparse matrices. Hand-chosen knots remain; the penalty slide removes counting knots as the main tuning problem.

A penalty replaces the knot count

Model

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(Y_i - \mu(x_i)\big)^2 + \lambda \int \big(\mu''(t)\big)^2\,\mathrm{d}t. \] The first term is fidelity. The integral is curvature. Here \(\lambda\) is the penalty weight, not a Poisson rate and not a relative intensity. Large \(\lambda\) forces a smooth curve. Small \(\lambda\) allows a wiggly one. A straight line has \(\mu'' = 0\), so the penalty does not forbid a bend. It discourages a bend the data do not support.

Read \(\lambda\) carefully against Poisson notation from the previous chapter. Large \(\lambda\) shrinks curvature; small \(\lambda\) chases the data. The straight-line remark prevents the myth that penalised splines cannot be linear—they can, when the data justify it.

The solution is a natural cubic spline

Theorem

The minimiser, over a suitable class of functions, is a natural cubic spline with a knot at every observed \(x_i\).

The same properties hold: piecewise cubic, knots at the distinct \(x_i\), continuous first and second derivatives, and linear beyond the extreme knots. An infinite-dimensional penalty problem becomes a finite spline. In matrix form it is penalised least squares, the ridge pattern \((\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{N} + \lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\).

Effective degrees of freedom are \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_\lambda)\). As \(\lambda \to \infty\) the fit approaches a straight line. As \(\lambda \to 0\) it approaches interpolation. GCV, with \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_\lambda)\) in the denominator, chooses \(\lambda\).

The theorem is the bridge from calculus to computation: one knot per distinct \(x_i\), yet a finite solve. Walk \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_\lambda)\) as effective df and tie \(\lambda\to\infty\) and \(\lambda\to 0\) to the bias–variance slide. Three knots, their usual start, sit at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of \(x\). Three to five knots is the range they recommend, and AIC can choose the number. A neighbourhood empties as dimension grows

Local methods need nearby observations. As the number of covariates grows, those neighbourhoods empty, and “local” stops meaning a small window. Naive multivariate smoothing breaks down. The practical compromise is an additive mean: one smooth per covariate, estimated as a one-dimensional problem.

A GAM is an additive smooth on the link

Model

A fraction spends the interactions

A full factorial of \(k\) two-level factors needs \(2^k\) runs. Ten factors need \(1024\). A fractional factorial keeps \(2^{k-p}\) runs. Five factors at a quarter fraction need \(2^{5-2} = 8\), against \(32\) for the full \(2^5\).

A definitive screen also sees a curve

Plackett–Burman is the classical screen: \(11\) two-level factors in \(12\) runs, with main effects aliased with two-factor interactions. NIST describes it as a design for when the main effects are the focus.

A definitive screening design is the modern alternative for continuous factors. The run count is about \(2k+1\), so \(14\) factors need about \(29\) runs rather than \(2^{14}\). Each factor has levels \(-1\), \(0\), and \(+1\), so a quadratic term \(\beta_{11} x^2\) can be seen. An optimal design is computed for the constraint

Bayesian fits and causal estimands

⚖️ Twenty minutes for the probability statement. Simpson, the DAG roles, and the network search are the reference stack.

The mean can stay; the probability statement changes

A Bayesian fit of the same GLM still uses

\[ g(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i \]

and a family for \(Y \mid x\). What changes is the status of \(\beta\).

  • the likelihood is the model for \(Y\) given \(\beta\)
  • the prior is what you are willing to say about \(\beta\) before these rows
  • the posterior is the updated distribution of \(\beta\) given the rows

Riley and colleagues note one use directly: a probability such as \(\Pr(\text{rate ratio} < 0.9 \mid \text{data})\) (Riley et al. 2022).

The GLM mean equation \(g(\mu_i)=\beta_0+\beta_1 x_i\) stays; what moves is whether \(\beta\) is fixed or uncertain. Walk the three bullets: likelihood for \(Y\mid\beta\), prior before the rows, posterior after. Riley’s example is a probability about a rate ratio, not a repeated-sampling interval—say that sentence aloud.

Three objects, in order

No prior is “non-informative” merely because it was the software default. A prior labelled vague can still move \(\hat\beta\) when the sample is small or the outcome is sparse. That check is a sensitivity analysis.

The overall rate is a weighted mean

The overall rate is the subgroup rates weighted by how many patients sit in each subgroup.

\[ \frac{800 \times 0.9 + 200 \times 0.7}{800 + 200} = 86\% \]

\[ \frac{200 \times 0.95 + 800 \times 0.75}{200 + 800} = 79\% \]

The denominators match. A puts a weight of \(n = 800\) on the light cases, where the rates are high. B puts a weight of \(n = 200\) on those same cases. That difference in weights is enough to reverse the overall ranking.

One independence, two causal stories

A directed acyclic graph is what lets you check a correlation pattern against a causal theory. One pattern can belong to more than one theory.

The shared statistical statement in his pair of examples: \(T\) is correlated with \(X\) and with \(Z\) whether or not you condition, and \(Z\) is conditionally independent of \(X\) given \(T\).

\(T\) is a common cause

\(T\) causes \(X\) and \(T\) causes \(Z\). There is no direct arrow between \(Z\) and \(X\). Conditioning on \(T\) leaves \(Z\) independent of \(X\).

\(Z\) acts through \(T\)

A third variable has a job

A variance factor touches only \(Y\)

\(Z\) causes \(Y\) and does not cause \(X\). One example is a batch effect. \(Z\) does not create a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). It can mask one. He would control it, by design or in the model, to recover power.

DAG: \(X\to Y\) and \(Z\to Y\) only—no \(Z\to X\). Batch effects are the named example: they inflate noise on \(Y\) without inducing \(X\)–\(Y\) correlation. Control for power, not because \(Z\) confounds the effect. Contrast with confounder (\(Z\to X\) and \(Z\to Y\)).

An instrument touches only \(X\)

\(Z\) causes \(X\) and does not cause \(Y\). One example is a genetic variant. \(Z\) does not create a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). It does not have to enter the regression. Some designs use it on purpose.

Instrument path: \(Z\to X\to Y\) with no arrow from \(Z\) to \(Y\). Genetic variant is Hertel’s example—\(Z\) need not be in the regression unless you are running an IV design. Students confuse instruments with confounders because both touch \(X\); check for a \(Z\to Y\) edge.

A collider is a common effect

\(X\) and \(Y\) both cause \(Z\). One example is inflammation. Controlling for \(Z\) induces a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). A study restricted to healthy people is the selection-bias version of the same arrow: the restriction can create correlations that were not there.

Do not control a collider. The word for doing it anyway is overmatching. The Bayesian-network notes call this arrow a v-structure, and they connect it to selection bias.

Collider: \(X\to Z\leftarrow Y\). Conditioning on \(Z\) opens a spurious \(X\)–\(Y\) association—block the path by not adjusting on \(Z\). Healthy-only restriction is selection bias with the same v-structure. Overmatching is the name for conditioning on a collider anyway.

A moderator changes the path

\(Z\) changes the causal path from \(X\) to \(Y\). One example is sex. Women and men may show different metabolite profiles in response to a disease.

The analysis is then a stratum, or an interaction. Matching on \(Z\) does not do that job, and the sample has to be large enough for the interaction.

Moderator \(Z\) changes how \(X\) maps to \(Y\)—sex and metabolite response is the slide example. You need an interaction or separate strata, not mere matching on \(Z\). Ask how this differs from a confounder (adjust to block) and a collider (do not adjust).

A collider: leave it out.

A variance factor: control it, for power.

A mediator: leave it out, unless the question is that path.

A moderator: an interaction or a stratum, not a match alone.

A direct link is not a direction

A knock-out supplies the direction

Time supplies the direction

The other source in the notes is a series. The regression is the dependency-network regression, moved forward one time step.

Model

\[ Y(t+1) \approx a_1 X_1(t) + \cdots + a_k X_k(t). \]

If \(a_i \ne 0\), then \(X_i\) regulates \(Y\) from one time to the next. The notes call this a dynamic Bayesian network: an initial state, the same transition from time to time, and the chain unrolled.

Read the model callout: \(Y(t+1)\) regressed on \(X_1(t),\ldots,X_k(t)\). Non-zero \(a_i\) means \(X_i\) regulates \(Y\) forward in time—direction from lag, not from partial correlation alone. Tests, or a score, when nobody states the graph

The notes’ three searches, for the case with no expert graph.

Constraint-based: test a conditional independence. If the test rejects independence, the nodes are joined.

Score-based: give each candidate graph a score for how well it describes the data, and maximise the score.

Hybrid: learn the skeleton from the constraints, then refine it with a score.

Marco Scutari’s benchmark, as the notes report it, for the developer of bnlearn: at small samples the constraint-based algorithms were more accurate than the score-based ones, and as accurate as the hybrids. Tabu search, a score-based method, was faster than the constraint-based algorithms and faster than simulated annealing.

Step three fragments: constraint-based joins nodes when independence tests fail; score-based maximises a graph score; hybrid skeleton then score. Scutari’s benchmark line compares accuracy at small \(n\) and Tabu search speed—bnlearn is named on the next slide. Stress these learn association structure under DAG assumptions, not causation by default.

A parameterised report is a function: one .qmd, several reports, and the thing that changes is a value in params.

  • A parameter is a string, a number, or a Boolean
  • A data frame has to be serialised to travel in, then rebuilt inside the document. The pointers are Dervieux and Helveston

For this room the natural parameters are a site, a year, or a warming level: one report per SPRUCE plot, or one per cropping system, from a single source file. Say that a parameter is not a substitute for a function; the computation should still live in a script that the document calls.

The default has to exist in the data

params:
  system: "SD1"

The parameter name is a column you will filter on. Any name is legal. If SD1 is not a level in the data, the code errors or the output is blank.

The same key swaps a site, a year, or a warming level without a second copy of the file.

Read it with params$

params is a list, so params$system is the string.

  • In a sentence, use inline R: a backtick, the letter r, then params$system
  • In a plot title, paste(params$system, "richness") or glue::glue("{params$system} richness")
  • Search the file for the hard-coded string and replace it with params$

Three ways to render a variant

  1. The Render button, or Ctrl+Shift+K, uses the defaults in the header
  2. From R, quarto::quarto_render() takes execute_params = list(system = "SD1")
  3. From the terminal, quarto render template.qmd -P system:'SD1'

The parameterised-reporting workshops cover rendering every variant in one go. The source of the talk is jadeynryan/slc-rug-quarto.

Several formats, still one file

format:
  html:
    embed-resources: true
  pdf: default
  docx: default
format-links: [pdf, docx]
  • embed-resources: true puts the HTML in one file
  • format-links chooses which formats the HTML page offers for download
  • format-links: false offers none
  • Give HTML and reveal.js different output-file names, or the second render overwrites the first

Use a tool, or write one

The practices in the rest of this block are from Nathalie Lehmann and Quentin Blampey, Good practices (2024). The second project shape is the one that needs them.

Both shapes end in a result somebody has to be able to rerun. The difference is who carries the maintenance: in the left-hand shape it is somebody else’s package, and in the right-hand one it is you. Most people in this room are in the left-hand column and still need the Git and dependency slides.

final_final is not a history

The name records a mood. It does not record which lines changed.

Read the four red questions aloud; they are the real content of this figure. Each one is a question a reviewer or a future you will ask, and none of them can be answered from a filename. Note that this is not a joke slide: the deck’s own history is a sequence of commits, which is how the figure numbers and the slide text stayed consistent.

Git answers the revision questions

  • which file made the figure in the article
  • what was deleted in the last version
  • which package versions the code used
  • why a small update for a revision changed the results

A commit is the answer to those four. A filename is not.

The hub is the shared copy

This deck’s hub is the GitHub repository named in the footer. The history of a slide is the history of the .qmd, not a second exported HTML.

Names, inputs, and the output

A function nobody else can read has three obligations:

  • a function name and argument names that say what they are
  • the input types, and the values that are allowed
  • a precise description of what comes back

data and x fail the first test. So does a function whose only documentation is its body.

The example in the source deck is a function called with an argument named “data”, which could be anything. Connect this to the codebook idea from the datasets block: a column name and a variable name carry the same obligation, which is to make the units and the allowed values visible without reading the implementation.

Put the documentation on a site

If you can, write the documentation as a site. The worked example is gustaveroussy.github.io/sopa, a GitHub Pages site for a package.

For an R package, pkgdown builds that site from the roxygen comments and the vignettes. GitHub Pages, or the gh-pages branch this deck already uses, is a place to put it.

Dependencies move after you publish

The package did not change. The ground under it did. A rerun on “whatever is current” is a different computation.

This is the slide that explains why a five-year-old analysis script fails today even though nobody touched it. The version you published against is a column in this picture, and the column moves. The practical consequence is on the lockfile slide: record the column, or the rerun is not the same computation.

An unkept package goes stale

If you do not maintain the code, it becomes deprecated.

Maintenance here is a decision about each new column of dependency versions:

  • test the release against them, or
  • state which column it still supports

Keep the old name until a stated date

def annotate_cell_types(X, d):
    warning("This function is deprecated and will be removed in Jan. 2025")
    return get_cell_types(X, markers=d)

The old name stays, warns, and forwards. The warning names the removal date.

The named example is Seurat, where renames across major versions broke a lot of downstream code. The remedy costs almost nothing: a shim, a warning, and a date. Note that the date is the part people omit, and it is the part that lets a user plan.

Release notes name the version

The example is v0.2.2, marked [0.2.2] - 2024-08-07:

  • a major line says the new disk format is backward compatible
  • a minor line adds an argument
  • a fixed line names a bug

The version number is the contract. The note is what the contract changed.

Record the column you tested

A version number is not enough if the dependency column is unnamed. Write down the versions that belonged to that release:

  • the numpy, pandas, and PyTorch numbers in the timeline
  • or renv.lock in an R project

The Posit::conf 2024 dev-ops materials keep that column in renv.lock. A later rerun then uses the lockfile, not today’s default install.

A test is code that checks a use

Testing means writing code that checks whether the package still does what it did.

A passing run is evidence about the cases you wrote down. It is the moment you can say the code is stable for those cases. A case you did not write is still unchecked.

The matrix shows which change broke

That grid, run on every change, is continuous integration.

The grid is the argument for automation in one picture. One row is a use case, one column is a version, and the cell is a pass or a fail. Nobody fills that grid in by hand across operating systems and language versions, which is why the next slide is a workflow file. The question printed under the grid is the one CI exists to answer.

Actions runs the tests for you

Open the log to see the failing case. Do not rerun the matrix by hand on a laptop and then forget which laptop. The example run is this Actions log.

A push to main can also publish

Continuous delivery is the same kind of file, with a deploy step at the end.

This deck’s .github/workflows/publish.yml runs on a push to main: it checks out the repository, installs Quarto, renders from the committed freeze, and publishes to gh-pages.

The getting-to-production workflow is the container version of the same pattern: on a push to main it builds an image and pushes it. The trigger is the same; the last step is a deploy instead of a test report.

Worth being concrete about this deck, because it is the live example in the room. The R code runs on a laptop, the results are committed under _freeze, and the Action installs Quarto only. That keeps the build fast and means a missing R package on the runner cannot break a published deck. The trade is that a changed chunk has to be re-rendered locally before the push.

Say which layer you are shipping

Before that deploy, separate three layers:

  • the presentation layer is what a person clicks
  • the processing layer is the work those clicks cause
  • the data layer is where the values are stored and read

A data update then changes the data layer. It does not require a new copy of the report, which is the Quarto path from the first slides.

The short list to take back

For any analysis:

  • an assert that refuses a bad input
  • a style guide, a formatter, and a linter
  • a pre-commit hook, so the check runs before the commit

For a package other people will use:

  • a dependency manager and a lockfile
  • tests on more than one operating system and language version
  • a documentation site, a release, and a version number you keep maintaining
