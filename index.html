What \(Y\) is in that table

\(Y\) is a count, or a share of a count.

  • a taxon abundance is a non-negative integer
  • a relative abundance is a composition: parts of a whole
  • many taxa are zero in many cores
  • the sampling variance is not a free Gaussian \(\sigma\)

Moisture, pH, soil chemistry, and organic carbon are the covariates we will want. They are companion measurements, not columns you should assume are already on this product.

Why a Gaussian lm is the wrong first tool

  • \(Y\) cannot be negative, and a Gaussian mean can predict negative counts
  • the variance of a count usually moves with the mean
  • a proportion is bounded, and an increase in one taxon is a decrease in the share of another
  • zeros are a feature of community tables, not a nuisance to delete

The deep dive after the pitfalls is this problem, with three likelihoods.

SPRUCE 🌲

A whole-ecosystem warming experiment on the S1 bog, Marcell Experimental Forest.

  • Question on the design page: how do plants, microbes, and ecosystem processes respond to warming, and how does elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) change that response?
  • Air and deep soil warmed from ambient to \(+9^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • Elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) atmospheres of about 800–900 ppm
  • Source: SPRUCE experimental design

All treatment numbers on the next slides are taken from that page, which cites Hanson et al. (2017) for the temperature differential and Cottingham et al. (2005) for regression designs. Do not expand those citations beyond what the page states. About two minutes.

Ten enclosures, not a two-group test

Ambient and elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\), each at \(+0\), \(+2.25\), \(+4.5\), \(+6.75\), and \(+9^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Plots 4, 10, 11, 16, and 19 receive elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\). Chamberless areas were also monitored.

What is measured, and what is independent

The response we will keep in view is a flux: soil \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) or \(\mathrm{CH}_4\).

  • the treatment is applied to the enclosure
  • collars and dates sit inside the enclosure
  • a weekly series is a repeated measure, not a new experimental unit
  • there are 10 enclosures in the warmed regression design

The model the design is asking for

For enclosure \(j\) and repeated measure \(i\),

\[ g(\mu_{ij}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1j} + \beta_2 x_{2j} + \beta_3 x_{1j} x_{2j} + \beta_4 \mathrm{season}_{ij} + u_j \]

  • \(x_{1j}\): warming assigned to the enclosure
  • \(x_{2j}\): elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) versus ambient
  • \(u_j\): enclosure-level departure, \(u_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2)\) under a random-intercept model

A \(t\)-test of “warm versus cool” is a different estimand, and a poorer one.

Point at the indices. j is the enclosure, i is the repeated observation. We return to u_j in the design block. About two minutes.

Six purposes, six different \(\mu\)

  • Describe a pattern
  • Compare groups
  • Explain an intervention
  • Predict a new unit
  • Explore and generate
  • Confirm a pre-specified claim

A significant coefficient does not say which of these you did.

Riley and colleagues ask authors to say whether the work is descriptive or causal, prognostic or predictive, exploratory or confirmatory. One minute.

From a slogan to a statistical question

Vague: “What is the effect of drought on forest carbon?”

Sharper, still one estimand:

  • difference in what? Net \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) flux, in stated units
  • between which conditions? Rain exclusion versus control
  • over which period, and on which population of sites?
  • at which unit? The plot that received the exclusion, not the technical replicate

Work one participant’s slogan into this shape if time allows. Two minutes.

The estimand is the target, not the software

The estimand is the quantity you claim to estimate.

  • a difference in tonnes of C per hectare
  • a flux ratio
  • a slope against warming
  • a mean over seasons, or a mean for one season
  • a site-specific effect, or an effect you want to transport to new sites

Changing the unit, the contrast, or the set of covariates changes the estimand. It is not a cosmetic choice of summary.

PICO, when the question is a comparison

For a designed contrast or a systematic comparison, name population, intervention or exposure, comparison, and outcome (Riley et al. 2022).

  1. Population or system, and the unit
  2. Intervention, exposure, or factor
  3. Comparison
  4. Outcome \(Y\), and the estimand in one clause

Outcome Mean soil \(\mathrm{CH}_4\) flux; estimand: slope on warming, and the warming \(\times \mathrm{CO}_2\) interaction

1 · Start with the question

A causal question asks what would change if the exposure changed. A prediction question asks how close \(\hat{\mu}\) is for a new unit. Write down which one you are answering.

2 · An effect, an interval, and a reason it matters

Riley and colleagues use a risk ratio of 0.97 (0.95 to 0.99) as a detected but small effect, and 0.70 (0.40 to 1.10) as a large but uncertain one (Riley et al. 2022).

Statistical detection is not a carbon consequence

Replace the clinical threshold with a flux, stock, or decision that would matter. Pre-state it when you can. The estimate and the interval stay in the sentence (Halsey et al., 2015).

3 · Missing data are part of the design

Report how much is missing, in \(Y\) and in \(x\), and what you did. A complete-case analysis is a strong assumption, not a default (Riley et al. 2022).

Three mechanisms

Figure credit on the source image: @daansan_ml.

The same idea as a graph

\(R\), the missingness indicator, has to be in the story. If \(R\) depends on the unobserved value of \(Y\), a model that only conditions on the observed covariates does not fix the bias.

4 · Do not dichotomise a continuous \(x\)

A threshold throws away order, spends power, and makes the result depend on the cut (Riley et al. 2022). Altman and Royston (2006) is the short note.

The same mistake in a carbon table

“Dry” versus “wet”, or soil temperature split at \(10^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

  • \(9.9^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(10.1^\circ\mathrm{C}\) become different regimes
  • \(10.1^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(25^\circ\mathrm{C}\) become the same regime
  • a second cut, chosen because it was “significant”, will not travel

Keep warming, moisture, and pH continuous unless the threshold is itself the scientific object.

5 · A straight line is an assumption

A one-unit step in \(x\) need not have the same effect at the bottom and the top of the range. Restricted cubic splines and fractional polynomials are the usual parametric checks (Riley et al. 2022).

A line can miss the hump

Illustrative simulation, seed 20261005. Not a SPRUCE fit. The clay line is \(\mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\) forced to be linear.

6 · A subgroup claim needs its own contrast

“Significant in fens, not in bogs” is not an interaction. Estimate the difference of effects, with an interval (Riley et al. 2022). Overlapping interval bars are not a test.

7 · \(I^2\) is not the size of the heterogeneity

\(I^2\) is a relative share of variance. Small studies can show a small \(I^2\) while the between-site variance is large. Report that variance. A regression on site means is not the core-level slope.

8 · A prediction needs three checks

Discrimination, calibration, and usefulness at a decision threshold are different questions (Riley et al. 2022). A large AUC with a miscalibrated probability still misleads the decision.

We will not fit a prediction model today. If your purpose is prediction, this card is the checklist.

9 · Repeated rows are not replicates

Ignoring the cluster pretends that rows in the same enclosure are as different as rows in different enclosures (Riley et al. 2022).

Count the unit before you count the rows

Teaching arithmetic, not a published study:

  • 4 sites, the level that received the treatment
  • 3 plots in each site
  • 20 cores in each plot
  • 3 technical readings on each core

\(4 \times 3 \times 20 \times 3 = 720\) rows. If the treatment was applied to the site, you do not have 720 independent replicates.

10 · Covariates are a scientific choice

For an explanatory model, screening on \(p\)-values is the wrong gate. Draw the paths first (Riley et al. 2022). Lasso and ridge answer a prediction problem; they are not a causal adjustment set.

Ridge penalises the squares of the coefficients (\(L_2\)). Lasso penalises the absolute values (\(L_1\)). Elastic net mixes the two.

Three arrows, three estimands

If the question is the effect of carbon on the taxon, moisture is a confounder: it feeds both. Adjusting for a mediator, or for a collider, answers a different question. We return to this in the causal block.

11 · Show that the conclusion can move

A constant hazard ratio, a “vague” prior, or a complete-case fit can each carry the result. Refit under a second defensible assumption and say what moved (Riley et al. 2022).

12 · Write so the analysis can be rerun

SAMPL, on the EQUATOR Network, is the checklist we will use at the end Lang and Altman, 2015.

Say the precise word

Prefer Avoid, unless you mean it
Median and quartiles, when \(Y\) is skewed Mean and SD as the only summary
Multivariable: several covariates Multivariate: several responses
Association, for an observational coefficient Effect, when nobody assigned \(x\)
The estimate and the interval “Significant” as the whole result
Quantile: the cut point Quantile: the group the cut creates

Where we go deeper

Of these twelve, the next hour stays with a problem the poster assumes you have already solved: the distribution of \(Y\).

Soil counts, fluxes, and compositions are often skewed, bounded, discrete, or full of zeros. A Gaussian linear model is then the wrong likelihood, even when the question, the estimand, and the design are clear.

When Y is not Gaussian

🔬 About seventy minutes. QQ plots, transformations, then three GLMs.

The shape is often the scientific object

Environmental \(Y\) is often:

  • skewed, and strictly positive
  • a count, or a composition
  • full of zeros, or censored at a detection limit
  • dependent in time or in space

A Gaussian lm can still be a useful approximation for a mean. It is a poor default when the support of \(Y\) makes the approximation impossible.

The assumption is conditional

A QQ plot of the raw column \(Y\) answers the wrong question.

The Gaussian linear model assumes that the conditional distribution \(Y \mid \mathbf{x}\) is Gaussian, with constant variance. In residual form,

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2). \]

Look at residuals, or at a residual QQ plot. Do not gate the analysis on a test of the marginal histogram.

How to read the plot 🔍

Points along the line support the reference shape. A curve, or a tail that leaves the line, is a departure. Illustrative residuals, seed 20261005.

A normality test is a poor gate

  • with a large \(n\), the test rejects a departure too small to change \(\hat{\mu}\)
  • with a small \(n\), it misses a departure large enough to matter
  • the \(p\)-value does not say whether the estimand is distorted
  • the test is aimed at the wrong object if you apply it to raw \(Y\) rather than to the model you will trust

Prefer the QQ plot, and then a likelihood that matches \(Y\).

A transformation can be the right scale

A transform is justified when it matches the science:

  • a multiplicative effect, so that logs are the natural contrast
  • a variance that grows with the mean, and a log that stabilises it
  • a physical rate that is already defined on a log scale

It is not justified by “the histogram looked skewed” alone.

\(\log Y\) changes the estimand

A Gaussian model for \(\log Y\) targets \(\mathbb{E}(\log Y \mid x)\).

\[ \exp\{\mathbb{E}(\log Y \mid x)\} \le \mathbb{E}(Y \mid x), \]

with equality only in degenerate cases. The left-hand side behaves like a geometric mean. The right-hand side is the arithmetic mean on the original scale.

\(\log(Y+1)\) is a third response. Zeros were the reason for the \(+1\), and they are still in the model, only hidden.

For counts, start from a distribution on the non-negative integers (O’Hara and Kotze, 2010; (Agresti 2015)).

Powers that chase a Gaussian shape

Yeo–Johnson chooses a power, including for zeros and negative values, so that the transformed sample is closer to Gaussian.

  • the criterion is closeness to a bell shape, not a scientific unit
  • the inverse transform is awkward, so a mean on the transformed scale is hard to say in the original unit
  • if you use it, describe the original \(Y\) with the median and the quartiles

It does not replace a likelihood whose support is the support of \(Y\).

Favour the model, not a pretest

A pretest that switches you from a \(t\)-test to a rank test still does not estimate \(\mu = \mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\). Rank tests answer a different contrast, and they have assumptions of their own.

The teaching sequence for the three models follows the SIB day on logistic regression and GLMs (slides, course).

The response chooses the family

Support of \(Y\) A first model
Continuous, unrestricted, stable variance Gaussian linear model
Two categories, truly binary Logit, \(Y \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi)\)
Non-negative integer, variance near the mean Poisson, log link
Same, with extra-Poisson variance Negative binomial, log link

Continuous flux is not on this list as a Poisson problem. A count of colonies, reads, or events is.

One skeleton for all three 🔗

  • Family: how \(\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x)\) depends on \(\mu\)
  • Link \(g\): the scale on which \(\beta_1 x\) is linear
  • Mean \(\mu = \mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\): what \(\beta\) describes, after you invert \(g\)

Agresti is the reference for the theory and the worked models (Agresti 2015). Estimation is by maximum likelihood, not by a normal test of \(Y\).

Quantile residuals check the family

These are randomised quantile residuals for a Poisson mean: if the family is right, they should look Gaussian. A heavy upper tail is a reason to leave the Poisson, not a reason to run Shapiro–Wilk on the raw counts.

Poisson · when the count matches the story

Use it when \(Y\) is a non-negative integer and, to a first approximation, \(\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x) = \mu\).

Colony counts on a plate are a clean example. Sequence-read totals usually are not: they are more variable than a Poisson, and they are compositional.

\[ \log \mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x. \]

Poisson · a simulated plate count

\(n = 120\) cores. \(x\) is centred soil moisture, in g/g, from \(-0.15\) to \(0.15\). \(Y\) is a colony count.

\[ Y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu), \qquad \log \mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x. \]

Fitted in this draw (seed 20261005): \(\hat\beta_0 = 2.03\), \(\hat\beta_1 = 2.05\). At \(x = 0\), \(\hat\mu = e^{2.03} \approx 7.6\) colonies.

This is not a NEON fit. The script is scripts/generate-slide-figures.R.

Poisson · the mean, with its band

The band is a Wald interval for \(\mu\), formed on the log scale and mapped back with \(\exp\). It is not a band for a new core’s count.

Poisson · what \(\hat\beta_1\) says

A step of \(+0.1\) g/g in centred moisture multiplies the mean count by

\[ \exp(0.1 \hat\beta_1) = 1.23 \]

Wald 95% interval in this draw: 1.14 to 1.32.

  • the contrast is multiplicative on \(\mu\), additive on \(\log \mu\)
  • a step of 1 g/g is outside this \(x\); do not quote \(\exp(\hat\beta_1) \approx 7.7\) as a moisture effect
  • if the variance exceeds the mean, this interval is too narrow

Negative binomial · the Poisson is too tight

Extra-Poisson variation is the usual state of ecological counts: unmeasured microsites, clumping, and noisy detection. The line on the plot is \(\mathrm{Var} = \mu\).

Negative binomial · same mean, one extra parameter

Keep the log link. Add a dispersion parameter \(\theta > 0\):

\[ \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{\theta}. \]

This is the MASS::glm.nb parameterisation: \(\theta\) large means “close to Poisson”. Agresti writes the same quadratic variance with a different symbol for the extra term (Agresti 2015).

In this second simulation, \(\hat\theta = 1.48\). The draws are not the Poisson points from the previous slide. The true mean was the same; the noise was not.

Negative binomial · the band that was missing

Green: uncertainty of \(\mu\). Clay: an approximate \(\mu \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SD}\) band from the fitted variance. The clay band is a sketch of the observation scale, not a calibrated prediction interval.

Negative binomial · read the mean, then \(\theta\)

For a \(+0.1\) g/g step, this draw gives a mean rate ratio

\[ \exp(0.1 \hat\beta_1) = 1.55 \]

Wald 95% interval: 1.29 to 1.87. Wider than the Poisson interval, because \(Y\) is more variable than \(\mu\).

  • \(\theta\) is not a second scientific effect; it describes leftover variation
  • the rate ratio is still a statement about \(\mu\), on the log link
  • a QQ plot of quantile residuals is the check, not a test that “selects NB”

Same count, three implementations

  • Poisson
  • Negative binomial
  • \(\log(Y+1)\), then Gaussian

\[ \log \mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x) = \mu. \]

Per \(+0.1\) g/g in this draw: rate ratio 1.23 (1.14 to 1.32).

\[ \log \mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x) = \mu + \mu^2/\theta. \]

A noisier draw, same true mean: rate ratio 1.55 (1.29 to 1.87), \(\hat\theta = 1.48\).

\[ \mathbb{E}\{\log(Y+1) \mid x\} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x. \]

That coefficient is not a rate ratio for \(\mu\). The two means are not the same function of \(x\).

Arrow keys step the tabs. Seed 20261005. None of the three is a NEON fit.

Logit · only if \(Y\) is really binary

Detection of a taxon in a core is binary. A flux cut at an arbitrary threshold is the dichotomisation we have already rejected.

\[ \mu = \pi = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid x). \]

The mean of a 0/1 response is the probability.

Logit · a simulated detection

\(x\) is centred pH, from \(-1.5\) to \(1.5\). \(Y\) is 1 if the taxon is detected.

\[ \mathrm{logit}(\pi) = \log \frac{\pi}{1-\pi} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x. \]

Fitted here: \(\hat\beta_0 = -0.32\), \(\hat\beta_1 = 1.44\).

At \(x = 0\), \(\hat\pi = \mathrm{logit}^{-1}(-0.32) \approx 0.42\).

Same seed, same script, still not a field dataset.

Logit · the curve is the result

The band is for \(\pi\), not for a single core. A core is still 0 or 1.

Logit · odds move by a factor, probabilities do not

A one-unit increase in centred pH multiplies the odds by

\[ \exp(\hat\beta_1) = 4.2 \]

Wald 95% interval: 2.4 to 7.4.

In this fit, \(\hat\pi\) goes from about 0.42 at \(x = 0\) to about 0.75 at \(x = 1\). That is a change of about 0.34 on the probability scale, not a 4.2-fold change in probability.

The interval is symmetric on the logit scale. It is not symmetric on the probability scale.

Hold the other covariates still

With several covariates,

\[ g(\mu) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots \]

\(\exp(\beta_1)\) is the multiplicative change in the mean, or in the odds, for a one-unit step in \(x_1\) with the other covariates held at the same values.

If \(x_2\) is a mediator or a collider, “held still” is a different estimand, not a more adjusted one.

What these three models do not cover

  • zero-inflation: a second process for structural zeros
  • compositions: a log-ratio, after a deliberate treatment of zeros
  • a continuous flux: often a positive continuous distribution, or a Gaussian model on a scale you can defend
  • dependence in space or in time: the design block is next

Quasi-Poisson is a variance fix, not a full likelihood. Use it only if you can say what the interval means.

Experimental design

🧮 Twenty minutes. The model has to inherit the plan.

The analysis inherits the plan

A mixed model does not repair an undescribed design. Before the formula, name:

  • the unit that received the treatment
  • the unit that was measured
  • what was randomised, blocked, or only observed
  • which rows are repeats of the same unit

Carbon data are often hierarchical, multi-site, and repeated in time. The random effect is the translation of that structure, not a decorative term.

A short map of plans

Plan What is randomised or assigned
Completely randomised Treatments to units, with no blocking factor
Randomised block Treatments inside a block that soaks up a nuisance gradient
Factorial Two or more factors crossed, so that interactions exist
Split-plot or strip-plot A hard-to-change factor on the large unit, another factor inside it
Regression or gradient A continuous driver, with enough levels to see the shape
Nested or hierarchical Units inside units, when crossing is impossible

Tanaka’s edibble slides are a precise way to write units and treatments down before you fit anything.

Completely randomised, then blocked

Completely randomised: every enclosure could have received every warming level, and you trust the site to be homogeneous.

Blocked: you group units that share a nuisance (a boardwalk, a water-table band, a day) and randomise inside the block.

SPRUCE’s design page says a block was considered, and that preliminary surveys did not show a gradient strong enough to require one. That is a design assumption, and it belongs in the report.

Factorial: the interaction is the point

Warming and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) are crossed. The scientific question on the design page is not only “does warming matter?” It is also “does elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) change the warming response?”

That second question is \(\beta_3\) in

\[ g(\mu_{ij}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1j} + \beta_2 x_{2j} + \beta_3 x_{1j} x_{2j} + \cdots \]

If you fit two separate slopes and then compare their \(p\)-values, you have skipped \(\beta_3\).

Split-plot: the large unit is hard to change

A factor that is expensive to reset is applied to the large unit. A factor that is easy to reset is applied inside it.

Cookie-dough batches and oven trays are the textbook version. In the field, an enclosure’s \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) setpoint and a collar’s measurement schedule are not interchangeable units.

The error term for the large-unit factor is the large-unit variance. Using the inner residual will make the interval too small.

Crossed, nested, or both

Warming and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) are crossed on the enclosure. Collars and dates are nested in the enclosure. A technical replicate nested in a core is a further level, and it is rarely the level of inference.

A random intercept is a distribution on \(u_j\)

Use a Gaussian equation for \(Y_{ij}\) only when \(Y\) can live on that scale. For a count or a detection, the cluster term sits on the link, as in the diagram.

\(u_j\) is a property of the cluster. It is not a covariate you forgot to measure, unless you are willing to say so.

Fixed and random name the target

  • Fixed: a contrast you want to report, such as the warming slope
  • Random: a level you want to generalise over, such as enclosures drawn from a bog
  • If you have the whole population of levels, a fixed indicator set is coherent
  • Ten enclosures is a small sample of a variance. \(\hat\sigma_u\) will be noisy

Partial pooling is the practical reason to bother: an enclosure with two bad weeks borrows strength from the others.

Repeated measures are a time series inside \(j\)

Do not run a separate test at each date and then narrate the dates that were “significant”.

A first model for a repeated flux:

  • a term for time, or for season
  • the treatment, which does not change inside the enclosure
  • the treatment \(\times\) time interaction, if the contrast itself changes
  • a dependence for the residuals, or a random intercept for the enclosure

Dates are \(i\). They do not become new experimental units because there are many of them.

SPRUCE chose a regression, not five bins

The design page records an earlier incomplete factorial, then a quantitative comparison: more temperature levels, used as a regression, resolved the shape better than a small ANOVA, at lower cost.

What was built:

  • five warming levels, from \(+0\) to \(+9^\circ\mathrm{C}\), steps of \(2.25^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • each level at ambient and at elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\)
  • ten enclosures, plus chamberless areas

The number of experimental units has to exceed the number of regression parameters you intend to estimate.

Where the replicate is

If you need… The replicate is…
The warming slope The enclosure, \(n = 10\)
A collar method variance Collars within an enclosure
A seasonal curve Dates within an enclosure, with dependence
A claim about other bogs Something this design does not contain

Pooling every collar-date into one residual answers none of these cleanly.

Do not bin the gradient you built

The design page allows a fallback: bin the ten enclosures into low, medium, and high for an ANOVA, if a variable forces it.

That fallback spends the design. Adjacent levels, \(+4.5\) and \(+6.75\), become either “the same” or “different” by a cut. The curve we already plotted is exactly what the binning throws away.

Keep \(x_1\) continuous. Report the slope per \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\), with an interval, and show the fitted mean.

Bayesian fits and causal estimands

⚖️ Twenty minutes. Same mean model, a different claim.

The mean can stay; the probability statement changes

A Bayesian fit of the same GLM still uses

\[ g(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i \]

and a family for \(Y \mid x\). What changes is the status of \(\beta\).

  • the likelihood is the model for \(Y\) given \(\beta\)
  • the prior is what you are willing to say about \(\beta\) before these rows
  • the posterior is the updated distribution of \(\beta\) given the rows

Riley and colleagues note one use directly: a probability such as \(\Pr(\text{rate ratio} < 0.9 \mid \text{data})\) (Riley et al. 2022).

Three objects, in order

No prior is “non-informative” merely because it was the software default. A prior labelled vague can still move \(\hat\beta\) when the sample is small or the outcome is sparse. That check is a sensitivity analysis.

What you are allowed to say

A Wald interval from glm is a statement about the sampling distribution of the estimator, under the model, if the experiment were repeated.

A posterior interval is a statement about \(\beta\) given this sample and this prior.

They can be numerically close for a large sample and a gentle prior. They are not the same sentence. Write the one you computed.

With ten enclosures, the prior on \(\sigma_u\) is not a detail. Refit with a second prior and show the slope again.

Where it earns its place this afternoon

  • partial pooling of sites or enclosures, with an honest prior on the variance
  • a parameter that must stay positive, such as a variance or a rate
  • a small sample, where a weakly identified \(\beta\) should not look precise
  • a hierarchical summary across studies, which is the meta-analysis version of the same idea

It does not remove confounding, and it does not choose the estimand.

A coefficient is not an intervention

SPRUCE assigns warming and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\). A slope on assigned warming estimates the effect of that assignment, in that bog, under the design assumptions.

An observational regression of taxon counts on organic carbon does not estimate “the effect of setting carbon to a new value”, unless the adjustment set matches the causal question.

Randomisation is a design fact. Adding covariates is not a substitute for it.

A confounder in the soil table

Both carbon and the taxon respond to moisture. The unadjusted slope of \(Y\) on carbon mixes the carbon path with the moisture path.

If the estimand is the association at a given moisture, moisture belongs in the model. If moisture is how carbon affects the taxon, moisture is a mediator, and adjusting for it removes part of the effect you wanted.

A mediator is a different question

The top path is indirect. The lower path is direct. The total effect of assigned warming leaves plant productivity alone. Conditioning on productivity estimates something else, and only under further assumptions.

Write the path you mean before you add the covariate.

Sensitivity is the causal report

For an observational claim, say what would have to be true for the conclusion to change:

  • a missing confounder associated with both \(x\) and \(Y\) at a stated strength
  • a different adjustment set from the same diagram
  • a different family, if the mean is sensitive to the variance assumption
  • a different prior, if the fit is Bayesian and the sample is small

SPRUCE still needs a sensitivity story for the chamber itself: the design page monitors chamberless areas because the enclosure is not identical to an open bog.

SAMPL, in workshop language 📝

Lang and Altman, SAMPL ask for the analysis to be explicit enough to audit. For this room, four lines are the minimum:

  1. Purpose: describe, compare, explain, predict, explore, or confirm
  2. Estimand, with the unit and the contrast
  3. Family, link, and what you did about missing rows
  4. The estimate, the interval, and the assumption you checked

“We used a mixed model” is not one of the four lines.

A checklist you can paste above a script

  1. Question and purpose, in one sentence
  2. Estimand, including the population and the unit
  3. Experimental or observational, and where the treatment was applied
  4. Support of \(Y\): continuous, count, binary, composition, censored
  5. Dependence: site, enclosure, date, space
  6. Missingness: how much, and a mechanism you are willing to defend
  7. Linearity of the linear predictor, checked, not assumed
  8. Family check: a QQ plot of the right residual
  9. Sensitivity: one alternative that could move the claim
  10. A report that separates association from an assigned effect
