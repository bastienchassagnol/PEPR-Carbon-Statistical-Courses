Inner product and norm

An inner product measures alignment. The norm is a length. StatHarbor Analytics drew these twelve panels.

Matrix multiplication

\(\eta = X\beta\) is the linear predictor. Each column of \(X\) is a covariate. Each row is one observation.

Least squares

Least squares minimises \(\|y - X\beta\|^2\). The residual is orthogonal to the columns of \(X\). That orthogonality is the projection from the notation block.

The hat matrix

\(P_X\) projects \(y\) onto the column space. When \(X\) has full column rank, \(P_X = X(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\), and \(\hat y = P_X y\).

A quadratic form

\(x^{\mathsf T}Ax\) is a squared length that depends on \(A\). A variance, and a Mahalanobis distance, are this shape.

Spectral decomposition

A symmetric matrix rotates to principal axes and then scales by its eigenvalues. Principal components are this decomposition of a covariance.

Singular values

A singular value decomposition does not need \(A\) to be square. It is the same geometry for a rectangular design matrix.

Positive semidefinite

A covariance is positive semidefinite: \(x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma x \ge 0\). Cholesky writes \(\Sigma = LL^{\mathsf T}\), which is how a simulation draws a correlated vector.

Score and information

The score is the gradient of the log-likelihood. Observed information is the curvature. More curvature means a more precise estimate.

A conditional covariance

The Schur complement is the covariance of one block after conditioning on the other. Conditioning on a covariate is this reduction.

The identities the other panels use

Transpose, inverse, trace, and \(\partial \|y-X\beta\|^2 / \partial \beta = -2X^{\mathsf T}(y-X\beta)\) are the working rules behind the estimator and the score.

Mahalanobis distance

\(d_M(x,\mu)^2 = (x-\mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\) measures distance after scale and correlation. The level sets are ellipses. Outlier rules that ignore \(\Sigma\) treat those ellipses as circles.

Two spreads, not one

\[ s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar Y)^2} \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar Y) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

\(s\) describes how far the observations sit from their mean. The standard error describes how far that mean would move if the sample were drawn again. Dividing by \(\sqrt{n}\) is the whole difference. In the draw on the next slides, the standard deviation of the 1,000 means is 2.11, against \(15/\sqrt{50} = 2.12\).

The draw is M. Fatih Tüzen’s: \(Y \sim N(100, 15)\), \(n = 50\), then 1,000 independent means. Seed 42 is his. It is not the workshop seed, and it is not a carbon series.

set.seed(42)

The mean moves less than the observations do

The left-hand spread is \(s\). The right-hand spread is the standard error. Variance, later on the map, is the same distinction.

Multivariable is not multivariate

Timothy Achala drew the distinction this way. Click the figure if the type is small. The wording we will police is say the precise word.

One outcome, or several

Multivariable: one \(Y\), several predictors. A logistic regression of detection on age, sex, body mass, and smoking is multivariable. It is not multivariate.

Multivariate: several responses, in one model, with the correlation between them. A multivariate probit, or a GEE for several binary outcomes. Systolic and diastolic pressure together, not as two separate fits.

Calling the first model “multivariate logistic regression” names a different analysis. Clinical papers often swap the words. ICH E9(R1) and CONSORT are the reason to keep them apart.

Functions and models

A model is a conditional distribution, \(P(Y \mid X)\), not a fitted curve treated as the process that made the data.

StatHarbor Analytics drew the ten rows. The next nine are the rest of that poster.

Derivatives, local change

A derivative is the local slope. The score is that slope for the log-likelihood, and it is what a small change in a parameter does to the fit.

Integrals and sums

An expectation, a marginal likelihood, and a sum of squared residuals are the same move: add the contributions.

Probability and distributions

\(P(X \in A)\) is a number attached to a set of outcomes. The distribution is the model of that uncertainty.

Expectation

The target is a functional of the distribution, \(\psi(P)\). In this workshop that functional is usually \(\mu = \mathbb{E}(Y \mid \mathbf{x})\), the conditional mean, not the raw rows.

Variance and uncertainty

Variance is dispersion. It is not bias, and it is not distance to the truth. The standard deviation and the standard error are the two scales that sentence has to keep apart.

Likelihood and optimisation

\(\hat\theta = \arg\max \ell(\theta)\) is the best fit relative to the likelihood and the criterion you chose. AIC and BIC change the criterion. They do not change the meaning of “best”.

Geometry and linear algebra

Least squares is a projection. The fitted vector is \(P_X y\), and the residual is orthogonal to the columns of \(X\). The symbols for that projection are the ones on the earlier slide.

Tangent space and local models

The directions in which the model can move, locally, are what an influence function and a standard error are built from.

Asymptotics

\(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta - \theta_0)\) settling toward \(N(0, V)\) is why a Wald interval shrinks. A large sample does not, by itself, remove bias or a misspecified mean. That limit is the large-sample fallacy in the pitfalls block.

What \(Y\) is in that table

\(Y\) is a count, or a share of a count.

  • a taxon abundance is a non-negative integer
  • a relative abundance is a composition: parts of a whole
  • many taxa are zero in many cores
  • the sampling variance is not a free Gaussian \(\sigma\)

Moisture, pH, soil chemistry, and organic carbon are the covariates we will want. They are companion measurements, not columns you should assume are already on this product. The symbols are on the notation slide.

Why a Gaussian lm is the wrong first tool

  • the variance of a count usually moves with the mean
  • a proportion is bounded, and an increase in one taxon is a decrease in the share of another

The model the design is asking for

The estimand is the target, not the software

The estimand is the quantity you claim to estimate.

  • a difference in tonnes of C per hectare
  • a flux ratio
  • a slope against warming
  • a mean over seasons, or a mean for one season
  • a site-specific effect, or an effect you want to transport to new sites

Changing the unit, the contrast, or the set of covariates changes the estimand. It is not a cosmetic choice of summary.

1 · Start with the question

A causal question asks what would change if the exposure changed. A prediction question asks how close \(\hat{\mu}\) is for a new unit. Write down which one you are answering. The comparison form is PICO.

Explain, predict, or confirm

  • Explain. Which covariates, and how large is β? The coefficient and its interval are the result.
  • Predict. How close is Ŷ on a unit the fit did not see? The score is a prediction error, not a p-value.
  • Confirm. The contrast, the covariates, and the claim were written down before the fit. A search until something is significant is exploratory.

MetricGate (April 2025) works the first two through a linear model and a random forest. Confirmatory is the pre-specification on the six purposes. The checks for a prediction are three different questions.

Two different scores

Prediction, on new units:

\[\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2}\]

Explanation, for one coefficient, under the linear model:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}, \qquad t = \hat\beta_j / \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j)\]

A small RMSE does not say that \(\hat\beta_j\) is the effect of changing \(x_j\).

A more flexible fit can predict worse

On their held-out rows, the linear model had RMSE 1.01 and the random forest 2.40.

The forest lost because the mean really was linear, and the noise covariates gave the trees something to chase. Complexity is not a better prediction. Interpretability is not a worse one either: the linear coefficients are the explanatory object, and here they also predicted better.

A hold-out score does not certify the coefficient

Cross-validation answers “how close is \(\hat{Y}\) on new rows?”. It does not say that \(\hat\beta\) is unbiased, or that the path from \(x\) to \(Y\) is the one you would intervene on.

Check the linear assumptions, and refit after removing influential rows, when the claim is about \(\beta\). Keep the hold-out for a prediction. Ranking, calibration, and the decision are three further checks.

A yes-or-no decision has two errors

Biswas recalls the two rates. A Type I error rejects the null when it is true. Its long-run rate, when the test is calibrated, is \(\alpha\). A Type II error fails to reject the null when it is false. Its rate is \(\beta\), and the power is \(1 - \beta\).

\(p\) is not that rate

His medication example is the right object. If the treatment has no effect, a \(p\) of 0.03 means that 3% of replicate studies would obtain an effect this large or larger.

Comparing that number with \(\alpha\) is the yes-or-no decision. The number itself is a probability about the data. The careful statement is the replicate definition.

Detection is not importance

Olszewski separates the two. Statistical evidence asks whether the data could have come from a world with no effect. Practical evidence is a threshold the investigators set, a minimal important difference. It is not a formula, and \(p\) does not supply it.

In a confirmatory analysis that threshold is written first. The estimate has to clear it, not merely clear zero. The next card is that contrast as two intervals.

2 · An effect, an interval, and a reason it matters

Riley and colleagues use a risk ratio of 0.97 (0.95 to 0.99) as a detected but small effect, and 0.70 (0.40 to 1.10) as a large but uncertain one (Riley et al. 2022).

Statistical detection versus environmental significance

Replace the clinical threshold with a flux, stock, or decision that would matter. Pre-state it when you can. The estimate and the interval stay in the sentence (Halsey et al. 2015). A small \(p\) is not that threshold. The next slides say what \(p\) is.

A p-value is about the data

Under identical replicates of this experiment, with the effect set to zero, \(p\) is the probability of a result more extreme than the one in hand (Harrell, ch. 22).

A \(p\) of 0.06 says that, under the model and the null, a replicate would be more extreme about 6 times in 100. It does not say that the null has probability 0.06.

α is a rate of false assertions

\(\alpha\) is the long-run probability of asserting an effect when the effect is exactly zero. It is not the probability that a positive result is false, and it is not “the probability of an error”.

A procedure that assumes the null cannot report the probability that the null is true. Fisher’s 1956 wording treated a 1% test as a 1% rate of mistaken decisions, which swaps \(P(\text{assert} \mid H_0)\) with \(P(H_0 \mid \text{assert})\) (Harrell).

The tail belongs to a model

Guillaume Rousselet takes a one-sample, one-sided \(t\) test: \(n = 50\), \(t = 1.52\), \(p = 0.0670\) when the imaginary experiments are normal, independent, of fixed size, and always analysed with that \(t\) test.

Sampling the same tail from the beta distribution the scores actually follow moves the simulated \(p\) from 0.0675 to 0.0804. Change the model and the number moves (garstats 2026).

Surprise, on a bit scale

\(-\log_2(0.05) = 4.32\). A \(p\) of 0.05 is the surprise of four heads in a row (Greenland, 2019).

Read \(p\) as compatibility between the model and the data, from 0 to 1, not as a verdict (Greenland et al., 2016).

One P, or a screen of them

Halsey and colleagues simulated a small normal experiment and showed a single \(P\) jumping across 0.05 from sample to sample. They called that fickleness a source of irreproducible decisions (Halsey et al. 2015).

van Helden repeated the simulation. The interval crossed the null in the same runs where \(P > 0.05\), so swapping the interval for \(P\) did not change the decision. He put the instability on the small sample, not on an intrinsic property of \(P\) (van Helden 2016).

Halsey, Curran-Everett, and Drummond replied that the shared mathematics was not the recommendation. The recommendation was to read the effect and its margin of error, rather than to re-apply a threshold (Halsey et al. 2016).

A planned question is not a screen

Huber called the exchange a clash of cultures (Huber 2016).

A craftsman reports one \(P\) for one question written in advance. An industrialist uses \(P\) to screen thousands of hypotheses, and then the histogram of all of them is part of the evidence.

One \(P\) can fall anywhere in \((0,1)\) when the null is true. The distribution of many \(P\) values is a different object. Both sides agree that \(P\) alone is an insufficient report.

The same p, a shrinking effect

Lantz keeps \(p = 0.0253\) while a preference for treatment X shrinks toward a coin toss. These are the two ends of his table.

The first would change a choice. The second would not. The interval is what a large sample tightens, which is why the large sample is still the better experiment. Put the estimate next to a pre-stated consequence. The opposite failure is a small study that still declares an effect: the size and the sign.

Five readings to retire

Lantz lists five things a \(p\)-value is not: the probability the result is due to chance; the probability a repeat will match; the probability the null is false; the probability the result matters; the probability it generalises.

The first three confuse a probability about the data with a probability about the hypothesis (the definition). The last two are the effect and the interval.

A small study can detect the wrong size

Gelman and Carlin: Type M is how many times too large a significant estimate is. Type S is how often that significant estimate has the wrong sign.

Those significant runs were about eleven times too big

Olszewski drew the previous figure from two normal samples, \(n = 10\) each, standard deviation 10, true difference 1, seed 1000, 10,000 \(t\) tests.

Power is about 5.5%. The significant estimates average about 11 times the true difference, and about a quarter to a third of them point the wrong way. A small \(p\) from a small study is a reason to doubt the size, which is why the effect stays in the sentence.

Power pulls the exaggeration down

On his curve the inflation is several-fold below about 10% power, and close to 1 once power passes about 80%. The same contrast is Lukas Vermeer’s simulator.

4 · Do not dichotomise a continuous \(x\)

A threshold throws away order, spends power, and makes the result depend on the cut (Riley et al. 2022). Altman and Royston (2006) is the short note.

The same mistake in a carbon table

“Dry” versus “wet”, or soil temperature split at \(10^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

  • \(9.9^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(10.1^\circ\mathrm{C}\) become different regimes
  • \(10.1^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(25^\circ\mathrm{C}\) become the same regime
  • a second cut, chosen because it was “significant”, will not travel

Keep warming, moisture, and pH continuous unless the threshold is itself the scientific object. SPRUCE kept the warming gradient: do not bin it.

6 · A subgroup claim needs its own contrast

“Significant in fens, not in bogs” is not an interaction. Estimate the difference of effects, with an interval (Riley et al. 2022). Overlapping interval bars are not a test.

7 · \(I^2\) is not the size of the heterogeneity

\(I^2\) is a relative share of variance. Small studies can show a small \(I^2\) while the between-site variance is large. Report that variance. A regression on site means is not the core-level slope. The arithmetic starts at one site, one estimate. Sampling noise shrinks with \(n\); a bias does not (random and systematic error).

One site, one estimate

In this block \(i\) is a study or a site, and \(y_i\) is that site’s estimated effect. It is not the core-level response \(Y\).

\[ y_i = \theta_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, s_i^2) \]

  • \(y_i\): the estimate you observe
  • \(\theta_i\): the true effect at that site
  • \(s_i^2\): the sampling variance of the estimate

Two sites with the same \(\theta\) can still disagree, because of \(\varepsilon_i\).

Say the change of index once. Here i is the site, which is the cluster. The core-level response is still Y, and it is not on this slide. About one minute. The next two slides separate that sampling noise from a bias that more rows will not remove.

Random error shrinks

Thiese, Ronna, and Ott define random error as noise around the true value, above and below it. It is not a bias. On the previous slide it is \(\varepsilon_i\), with variance \(s_i^2\). A larger sample at that site makes \(s_i\) smaller, which is the same move as the standard error.

Selection, a misread instrument, and a confounder are not this noise. They shift every row the same way.

A larger sample does not shrink a bias

Their example is a scale that adds the same offset to every person. More people do not remove it. Figure 1 in that paper, adapted from Rothman, is this shape. The curves are illustrative, from scripts/generate-slide-figures.R.

Fixed effect, or random effects

  • Fixed effect
  • Random effects

\[\theta_i = \mu, \qquad w_i^{\mathrm{FE}} = 1/s_i^2, \qquad \tau^2 = 0\]

The \(y_i\) differ only by sampling. The pooled mean weights each site by \(w_i^{\mathrm{FE}}\).

\[\theta_i = \mu + u_i, \quad u_i \sim N(0, \tau^2), \qquad y_i \sim N(\mu,\, s_i^2 + \tau^2)\]

The same two lines are a hierarchical model. In the design block: fixed and random name the target.

Arrow keys move between the tabs. Fixed effect: one theta. Random effects: the thetas are draws. Do not say the random-effects mean is automatically the causal effect. One minute on each tab. The design-block version is the slide “fixed and random name the target”.

\(\tau^2\) is how far the true effects spread

\[ \theta_i \sim N(\mu, \tau^2) \]

\(\tau^2\) is on the squared scale of the effect. It is not a percentage.

Arithmetic, not a carbon dataset: \(\tau^2 = 0.09\) on a log risk ratio gives \(\tau = 0.30\), so a rough band for the site effects is \(\mu \pm 1.96\tau \approx \mu \pm 0.59\). Call that large or small only after you name the scale.

A precise site with \(s_i^2 = 0.01\) has fixed-effect weight \(100\). With \(\tau^2 = 0.09\) the random-effects weight is \(1/(0.01+0.09) = 10\). That site no longer dominates the pool.

Stress the units. Then the weight: heterogeneity stops the most precise site from deciding the mean alone.

\(I^2\) compares that spread with the noise

If the sampling variances are similar,

\[ I^2 \approx \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + s^2}. \]

The usual estimator starts from Cochran’s \(Q\), the weighted sum of squared departures from the fixed-effect mean:

\[ Q = \sum_i \frac{1}{s_i^2}\,(y_i - \hat\mu_{\mathrm{FE}})^2, \qquad I^2 = \max\left(0,\, \frac{Q - (k-1)}{Q}\right). \]

Arithmetic: \(Q = 40\) and \(k = 11\) studies give \(I^2 = (40-10)/40 = 75\%\). \(Q\) asks whether the scatter exceeds sampling error. \(I^2\) turns the excess into a share of \(Q\). Neither replaces \(\tau^2\).

Write Q, then I-squared, on the board if someone treats 75 percent as a large tau. Under no heterogeneity, Q is about k minus 1.

The same \(\tau^2\) can produce either \(I^2\)

Keep \(\tau^2 = 0.04\).

  • Noisy sites, \(s^2 = 0.16\): \(I^2 = 0.04/0.20 = 20\%\)
  • Precise sites, \(s^2 = 0.0025\): \(I^2 = 0.04/0.0425 \approx 94\%\)

The true effects did not move. The measurements got sharper, so heterogeneity is a larger share of what you see.

Random effects are already hierarchical

\(\mu, \tau^2 \rightarrow \theta_i \rightarrow y_i\). The \(\theta_i\) are the site-level random effects. Their variance is \(\tau^2\).

A moderator leaves a residual \(\tau^2\)

Discrimination is the ranking

Teaching arithmetic, not a fitted model: 8 of 10 pairs ordered correctly is an AUC of 0.8. The diagonal is 0.5.

Teaching arithmetic: \(\hat\pi = 0.80\) on 10 sites, and the event on only 3 of them, is a miscalibrated bin. The ranks can still be right.

Utility is whether the score changes the action

At a threshold \(t\), net benefit charges each false action at the odds \(t/(1-t)\):

\(t\) need not be clinical. It is the chance of a peat source, or of a stock change, at which a wasted visit costs as much as a missed one. A useful score beats both “act on every site” and “act on none” on a band of \(t\).

When Y is not Gaussian

🔬 About seventy minutes. QQ plots, transformations, three GLMs, then a smooth mean.

The pipeline rewrites the column first

Hartmann and Rudolph, EGU 2026 short course SC2.28, draw the usual order. The circled step is where the score is judged. A rewrite of the column that changes the mean shows up there as a number you cannot interpret. This hour is about that rewrite.

The shape is often the scientific object

Environmental \(Y\) is often:

  • skewed, and strictly positive
  • a count, or a composition
  • full of zeros, or censored at a detection limit
  • dependent in time or in space

A Gaussian lm can still be a useful approximation for a mean. It is a poor default when the support of \(Y\) makes the approximation impossible. This hour follows where we go deeper. Skew, in particular, is ordinary: Quetilismus.

Quetilismus

Adrian Olszewski names the habit after Edgeworth’s word for Quetelet. A unimodal curve is treated as a normal curve. Skew is then a defect to repair: drop the tails, wait for a larger sample, or transform until the histogram looks symmetric.

The 2KMM note of 15 May 2023 and the PDF of that essay are the same text. The spelling-cleaned copy is the Medium post of 2 January 2026.

A product is not a sum

The classical central limit theorem is a statement about a sum, and it needs a finite variance. A product of positive steps is a different limit. Cascades, growth, and decay land on a log-normal, which is skewed. Cauchy, Lévy, and Pareto sit outside that classical sum.

Species abundance and environmental concentrations are on the product side (Limpert, Stahel, and Abbt, 2001; Andersson, 2021). More rows do not turn a product into a sum.

Skew is a description

His summary, from the opening of the Medium post: a skewed description of a real process does not become less legitimate because the sample grew. Treating the normal law as the dominant description sets aside the mechanisms that generate the skew.

The next slide is the modelling consequence. The Gaussian assumption, when it is used, is about \(Y \mid \mathbf{x}\), not about the raw histogram.

The assumption is conditional

A QQ plot of the raw column \(Y\) answers the wrong question.

The Gaussian linear model assumes that the conditional distribution \(Y \mid \mathbf{x}\) is Gaussian, with constant variance. In residual form,

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2). \]

Look at residuals, or at a residual QQ plot. Do not gate the analysis on a test of the marginal histogram.

The ends are allowed to wander

Rodrigue: the horizontal axis is the quantile under the reference distribution. The vertical axis is the observed quantile. A straight line, with extra scatter at the ends, is the reference. Order statistics are noisier in the tails. A systematic curve is the departure. The named shapes are the shape figures.

A normality test is a poor gate

  • with a large \(n\), the test rejects a departure too small to change \(\hat{\mu}\)
  • with a small \(n\), it misses a departure large enough to matter
  • the \(p\)-value does not say whether the estimand is distorted
  • the test is aimed at the wrong object if you apply it to raw \(Y\) rather than to the model you will trust

Prefer the QQ plot, and then a likelihood that matches \(Y\). A formal test is a poor substitute for that plot.

Two sample sizes, two failures

Adrian Olszewski’s rule, which this hour follows: stop using them, except when a reviewer demands the number. He counts more than 90 procedures.

  • A small sample leaves them underpowered, so a real departure often passes.
  • Past about 150–200 observations they reject almost any departure, including one too small to matter, while the central limit theorem for a mean is improving.
  • Families aimed at skew, at kurtosis, and at the whole distribution contradict one another. Shapiro–Wilk is one of those families, not a universal test.

The same sample, opposite answers

\(n = 400\), skewness about 0, kurtosis about 3. Shapiro–Wilk gives \(p = 0.123\). Lilliefors, Cramér–von Mises, and Anderson–Darling reject. The QQ plot shows the step either way.

Three means, three questions

Sharmi Murali: a sum, a product, or a reciprocal. \(A \ge G \ge H\) for positive numbers, with equality only when every value is the same. A product process wants \(G\), and logging \(Y\) changes the estimand to that mean.

A box plot hides the shape

A box plot reports a median, a box, and whiskers. It does not show a second mode, a pile of ties, or how many points sit in the tail. The case against using it alone is that those are often the features a carbon response actually has.

Prefer a plot that keeps the raw points. A raincloud adds a density and a short summary beside them (Scherer, 6 June 2021).

Four questions, not one test

Olszewski groups comparisons of two or more samples by the question, from the most general to the most specific. About nine hundred named procedures sit in these groups. The name of the procedure is not the hypothesis.

Whole distributions, then the tails

A test of the empirical distribution functions asks only whether \(F = G\). It does not say whether the difference is a shift, a scale, or a shape.

Olszewski’s family: Kolmogorov–Smirnov uses the largest vertical gap, and it is the least sensitive of the three. Cramér–von Mises integrates the squared gap, so a small gap that persists can count. Anderson–Darling uses the same integral with extra weight in the tails. For a heavy-tailed flux, Anderson–Darling is the one that looks where the mass of the question sits.

A rank test is not a mean test

Mann–Whitney estimates \(P(B>A)+\tfrac12 P(B=A)\). Equal medians can be decisive. A gap of 10 can be invisible. For a median, use quantile regression.

Ranks answer a different question

Unless the two distributions differ only by a shift, a rank test mixes location, scale, and shape. Mann–Whitney can be small when the means are equal and the variances are not, and large when the means differ and the variances differ the other way. That is Studentization: the null of a rank test is not the null of a mean.

If the planned question is a mean, stay with a procedure for a mean: a permutation Welch test, Freedman–Lane, or a wild bootstrap. Brunner–Munzel is the rank procedure that allows unequal variances, and it still estimates a probability, not \(\mu\). The map of those choices is the six columns.

Six routes, one of them a mean

The columns are questions. Mann–Whitney is the second, not a median. The last column is still a mean: permutation Welch, Freedman–Lane, a wild bootstrap.

Keep the mean, and change the standard error

Olszewski keeps the mean when that is the question. Welch does not need a pretest of the variances. Yuen changes the target to a trimmed mean. A permutation Welch test keeps the mean when the distribution is not Gaussian.

The same mean, with a covariate

Adding a covariate does not force a rank test. Ordinary least squares is the ANCOVA when the variances match and the slopes are parallel. Unequal variances move the standard error to a sandwich estimator or to generalised least squares. A non-Gaussian residual moves the test to Freedman–Lane, or to a wild bootstrap, still for the mean.

Prefer Welch when the model is this simple

Start from Welch (Olszewski). The pooled test forces a common variance. In his sketch the estimate is \(-2.99\) either way; the \(p\)-values are 0.049 and 0.052.

A bell curve is not a feature test

Hartmann and Rudolph. The 2-sigma interval is 92 ± 24%, so it leaves 0–100. The bell beside it is the 68%, 95%, 99.7% picture. A normality test does not turn that interval into a recovery rate. The same objection applies to a concentration, a count, or a composition.

The excuse is not a diagnosis

The same slides list the defences of that summary. Everybody reports it this way. The variance was large. (n) was too small for anything more accurate. It is the reporting standard. One only wanted the mean and the variability. The statistically careful version made the story disappear.

None of these is a reason to attach a Gaussian spread to a feature whose scale forbids it. The QQ plot shows the shape. It does not choose the summary.

Before you transform \(Y\)

  1. Why is transforming the response usually a bad default?
  2. What do you do instead when a Gaussian model for \(Y\) does not fit?

Olszewski’s answers, from the 2022 note, follow the variance-stabilising case. A multiplicative process is the case he keeps. A skewed histogram is not.

A transformation can be the right scale

A transform is justified when it matches the science:

  • a multiplicative effect, so that logs are the natural contrast
  • a variance that grows with the mean, and a log that stabilises it
  • a physical rate that is already defined on a log scale

It is not justified by “the histogram looked skewed” alone. What that shortcut costs starts at a transform rewrites the question.

Match the transform to \(v(\mu)\)

Huber, chapter 13, writes \(v(\mu)\) for the way the variance trends with the mean. Three prototypes cover most assays:

  • constant: \(v(\mu) = c\)
  • Poisson-like: \(v(\mu) = a\mu\)
  • quadratic: the standard deviation proportional to the mean, so \(v(\mu) = b\mu^2\)

A real measurement can add them. For microarray fluorescence he cites Rocke and Durbin (2001): background plus a multiplicative noise, \(v(\mu) = b\mu^2 + c\). A variance-stabilising transformation is built from that curve, so replicate spread no longer tracks the mean. One power for the whole sample, as on the Yeo–Johnson slide, does not use \(v(\mu)\).

A VST is for a Euclidean next step

Huber’s reasons for analysing the transformed scale:

a plot, or a heatmap, that spends its ink across the range

PCA, or a clustering that uses correlation, no longer dominated by a few very large means

a method that assumes identical, homoskedastic noise

That is the machine-learning case: a method whose geometry is Euclidean and wants a roughly constant spread. The transformation is still a new response. Inference for \(\mu\) on the original support stays with the link.

A transform rewrites the question

A logarithm changes a difference of arithmetic means into a ratio of geometric means. A Box–Cox power such as \(-0.67\) is not a unit anyone measured.

On \(\log Y\) against \(x\), a small coefficient is nearly a percentage: \(\exp(0.05) \approx 1.05\). A coefficient of \(0.5\) is not a 50% change, because \(\exp(0.5) \approx 1.65\). The inequality that separates the two means is written later.

The variance travels with the mean

In a Gaussian model the mean and the variance are separate parameters. After a log they are tied: a log-normal variance depends on the mean. A power chosen only to stabilise the spread changes that relationship even when the power is not a logarithm.

The interval does not travel back

\[ g\{\mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\} \ne \mathbb{E}\{g(Y) \mid x\} \]

unless \(g\) is linear. A confidence interval, a moment, or a test computed on \(g(Y)\) is not the same object on the original scale after you apply \(g^{-1}\). A \(p\)-value on the transformed response and an interval on the original scale can disagree about the claim. The log case is the estimand slide.

A logarithm needs a positive response

\(\log Y\) is undefined when \(Y\) can be negative. Adding a constant, as in \(\log(Y + c)\), defines a different response. A bone-density T-score is one measurement that is allowed to be negative. Quantile regression does not need the sign. Yeo–Johnson is the power that allows negatives, and the later slide says what that power costs.

The pooled histogram is the wrong picture

Conditional Gaussian groups with different means look skewed once you pool them. In his sketch the groups are normal, seed 1000, and the model is lm(DV ~ IV). Logging the pooled \(Y\) because the histogram is skewed spoils residuals that were already close to Gaussian.

Transform when the conditional distribution is skewed. That is what the residual plot checks. If \(\mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\) is already approximately Gaussian, the transform is not the remedy.

Or model the variance, or change the summary

Generalised least squares or estimating equations when the variance or the correlation is the problem, and the mean is still the target.

Quantile regression when the target is a quantile, including a median, and including censored \(Y\).

A robust or rank procedure when the question is not an arithmetic mean. The map is that choice. A permutation Welch test is the choice when the mean remains the question.

\(\log Y\) changes the estimand

A Gaussian model for \(\log Y\) targets \(\mathbb{E}(\log Y \mid x)\).

\[ \exp\{\mathbb{E}(\log Y \mid x)\} \le \mathbb{E}(Y \mid x), \]

with equality only in degenerate cases. The left-hand side behaves like a geometric mean. The right-hand side is the arithmetic mean on the original scale.

\(\log(Y+1)\) is a third response. Zeros were the reason for the \(+1\), and they are still in the model, only hidden.

For counts, start from a distribution on the non-negative integers (O’Hara and Kotze, 2010; (Agresti 2015)).

Powers that chase a Gaussian shape

Yeo–Johnson chooses a power, including for zeros and negative values, so that the transformed sample is closer to Gaussian. When the next method is Euclidean, such as a PCA, the transform that follows \(v(\mu)\) is the variance-stabilising transformation.

  • the criterion is closeness to a bell shape, not a scientific unit
  • the inverse transform is awkward, so a mean on the transformed scale is hard to say in the original unit
  • if you use it, describe the original \(Y\) with the median and the quartiles

It does not replace a likelihood whose support is the support of \(Y\).

A test is a model with one factor

Lindeløv writes the common tests as linear models. A Pearson correlation is the slope in lm(scale(y) ~ scale(x)): standardising both variables makes that slope equal to \(r\). A \(\chi^2\) test of counts is a Poisson log-linear model, glm(counts ~ group, family = poisson), compared with an intercept-only model.

The model is the more flexible object. It keeps the same contrast and adds a covariate, a variance weight, or a link. A named test usually cannot.

A paired \(t\) reports the mean

Loïc, seed 1234, 100 people measured twice. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives \(V = 1711\) and \(p = 0.005\). The paired \(t\) test gives a mean difference of \(-0.42\), an interval from \(-0.69\) to \(-0.14\), and \(p = 0.003\). The sign is the order of the two levels.

Both reject. Only the paired \(t\) test says how large the mean change is. The signed-rank test is a statement about the ranks of the within-person differences.

The pair is a random intercept

An ordinary lm on the same rows, without the person, keeps the estimate at \(0.42\) and inflates the standard error to \(0.28\), so \(p = 0.14\). A random intercept, (1 | ind), keeps \(0.42\) and brings the standard error back to \(0.14\). The \(t\) statistic returns to \(2.99\), the paired test.

In that fit the person-level variance is \(3.06\) and the residual variance is \(0.96\). Dropping the pair does not change the estimate. It charges the within-person contrast with the between-person noise.

When ordinary least squares is the wrong skeleton

If the Gaussian, independent, constant-variance assumptions fail, the replacements are still models.

  • Generalised least squares: a known pattern for the variance, which is the Welch test when the pattern is a group.
  • Generalised estimating equations: a working correlation and a sandwich standard error.
  • A generalised linear model: a distribution for \(Y\) and a link for \(\mu\).
  • A generalised additive model: a smooth function of \(x\) in place of a straight line.

This hour then stays with the generalised linear model. The others are named so a later choice is a choice of estimand, not a new religion.

aov() is Type I

Olszewski: aov() is Type I, so the treatment \(F\) in his sketch is 16.6 or 38.7 depending on order. car::Anova(type = 2) does not inherit that order.

Three types, then a deviance

Type I tests each term after the terms written before it. Type II tests each main effect after the other main effects, and an interaction after the mains. Type III tests each term after every other term, including interactions. The three answer different questions. Pick one before fitting, and do not use Type I unless the order is the scientific order.

For a generalised linear model the same partition is an analysis of deviance. The statistic is a likelihood-ratio \(\chi^2\), or a score test, rather than an \(F\) on residual sums of squares. anova(glm(...), test = "LRT") is that comparison of nested models. It is still a test of a term in a model, not a new model. His 2025 notes put the conditional mean, the probability, the quantile, and the hazard on one page as the functions that comparison can target.

Favour the model, not a pretest

A pretest that switches you from a \(t\)-test to a rank test still does not estimate \(\mu = \mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\). Rank tests answer a different contrast, and they have assumptions of their own.

One row, then every row

For one observation,

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_{p-1} x_{i,p-1} + \varepsilon_i. \]

Stacked, that is ( = + ). The first column of () is 1 when the model has an intercept. Least squares chooses () to minimise the sum of squared vertical residuals. The fitted values are ( = ), and () is the hat matrix.

Ordinary least squares is BLUE

The Gauss–Markov theorem: if ((_i) = 0), the (_i) are uncorrelated, each has the same finite variance (^2), and () has full column rank, then

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y} \]

has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators. The errors do not have to be Gaussian for that result. Gauss used normality. Markov removed it.

If, in addition, the (_i) are independent and Gaussian, that same () is the maximum-likelihood estimator. The (t) tests and the (F) test on the printout use this extra assumption. If the variances differ or the errors are correlated, the corresponding estimator is generalised least squares, which is the Welch case when the variance is a group.

A coefficient is a (t)

summary(lm) tests each coefficient against 0. The statistic is the estimate divided by its standard error. Angelino and Marcone note that this test assumes Gaussian residuals. It is the interesting test for a slope. It is rarely the question for the intercept.

In a model with several covariates, each line is the test of that coefficient with the other terms already in the model. Mahanty (9 January 2018) maps the same printout. Two of his readings do not travel: multiple (R) is not a count of points on the line, and a small (p) is not a verdict that the model fits.

The (F) test compares two nested models

The (F) statistic on the same printout compares the fitted model with the intercept-only model. Angelino and Marcone write it as the drop in the residual sum of squares, per extra parameter, divided by the remaining residual mean square:

\[ \frac{\{\mathrm{SS}(\mathrm{mean}) - \mathrm{SS}(\mathrm{fit})\}/(p_{\mathrm{fit}} - p_{\mathrm{mean}})}{\mathrm{SS}(\mathrm{fit})/(n - p_{\mathrm{fit}})}. \]

With a single slope, this (F) matches the (t) test of that slope. With several slopes, every coefficient can be quiet and the (F) test can still reject. (R^2) is the share of the variance of (Y) that the fit captures. Adjusted (R^2) charges a cost for each extra term. The residual degrees of freedom are (n) minus the number of estimated parameters.

The link acts on \(\mu\), not on \(Y\)

Agresti writes a generalised linear model as one object: a distribution for \(Y\), a linear predictor, and a link on the conditional mean (Agresti 2015).

Model

\[ g(\mu_i) = \eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \qquad \mu_i = \mathbb{E}(Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i). \]

A transformation route instead fits \(g(Y_i) = \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i\). The first line keeps the estimand on \(\mu\). The second rewrites each observation before the fit.

After the fit, \(\mathbb{E}(Y \mid \mathbf{x}) = g^{-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is a mean on the scale you measured: a probability, an expected count, a positive rate. You do not model \(g(Y)\) and then argue backwards to \(\mathbb{E}(Y)\). The estimand slides are what that argument costs.

\(E\{g(Y)\}\) is not \(g\{E(Y)\}\)

For a nonlinear \(g\),

\[ \mathbb{E}\{g(Y) \mid \mathbf{x}\} \ne g\{\mathbb{E}(Y \mid \mathbf{x})\}. \]

Regressing \(\log Y\) targets \(\mathbb{E}(\log Y \mid \mathbf{x})\). A log link targets \(g(\mu) = \log \mu\). In general \(\exp\{\mathbb{E}(\log Y \mid \mathbf{x})\} \ne \mathbb{E}(Y \mid \mathbf{x})\). A transformation model and a log-link GLM are different sentences about \(Y\).

The family matches the type of \(Y\)

Ordinary least squares on a transformed response still carries a Gaussian error on whatever scale you chose.

A GLM keeps \(Y\) on its support and changes the family:

  • Bernoulli or binomial for a 0/1 outcome or a proportion
  • Poisson for a non-negative integer count
  • Gaussian on the identity link when that support is appropriate

The family table is the first menu. The point here is that the likelihood is written for \(Y\), not for \(g(Y)\).

Variance travels with \(\mu\)

Classical linear regression often assumes

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma^2. \]

In a GLM the variance is tied to the mean through the family:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \phi\, V(\mu_i). \]

Poisson: \(V(\mu) = \mu\). Binomial with mean \(\pi\): variance \(\pi(1-\pi)\). That mean–variance link is part of the model, not a post hoc fix on the residuals. When the variance rises with the mean, scale against location was already warning you.

The link keeps \(\mu\) in range

\(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) can be any real number. The inverse link maps it into the support of \(\mu\).

  • \(\operatorname{logit}(\pi) = \mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) forces \(0 < \pi < 1\)
  • \(\log(\mu) = \mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) forces \(\mu > 0\)

A straight line on a transformed \(Y\) does not give the same guarantee on the mean of the original response. That is why the link stays on the mean, not on \(Y\).

Coefficients read on the link scale

The link is chosen so \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) answers a substantive question on the mean.

For \(\log(\mu) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\),

\[ \frac{\mu(x+1)}{\mu(x)} = e^{\beta_1}. \]

For a logit on \(\pi\), \(e^{\beta_1}\) is an odds ratio. The goal is not to make a histogram look Gaussian. The goal is a coefficient that names an effect on \(\mu\). The compact contrast is the link stays on the mean.

A continuous response, on the identity

Angelino and Marcone send a continuous response to a linear regression. The drawing is Gaussian, and the link is the identity, so \(\mu\) and the linear predictor are the same number.

A binary response, on the logit

A binary response goes to a logistic regression. Here \(\mu = \pi = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid x)\). The logit is the link they use. The probit is the alternative they mention later, and it stays close to this curve.

A count, on the log

A count goes to a Poisson regression. They write the mean as \(\lambda\). That symbol is \(\mu\) on these slides. The log link keeps \(\mu > 0\), and \(\exp(\beta_1)\) is a multiplicative change in the mean count.

Their four working assumptions: \(Y\) is a count per unit of time or space; \(\log \mu\) is linear in \(x\); the observations are independent; and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x) = \mu\).

The fit maximises the likelihood

Angelino and Marcone estimate by maximum likelihood. For a Gaussian linear model that maximiser is the least-squares fit. For the logit and the Poisson log link they say there is no closed form: the estimates come from an iterative algorithm.

The Newton step for the logit, from the chapter embedded below, uses the Bernoulli variances as weights:

\[ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)} + (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{(t)}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(t)}), \qquad w_i = \pi_i(1-\pi_i). \]

Angelino and Marcone state that the maximiser is iterative and do not write the update. The displayed step is Burakov’s equation (2.87), in the chapter embedded later. \(\mathbf{W}\) is diagonal. At convergence, \((\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) is the estimated covariance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\).

Three residual scales

Angelino and Marcone separate three residuals after a GLM.

A deviance residual, plotted against the fitted value and against each covariate. A curve is a missing piece of the linear predictor.

A Pearson residual, \((Y - \mu)\) divided by the fitted standard deviation. A trend against a covariate is a term the model does not yet contain.

Deviance is the residual sum of squares

Angelino and Marcone measure lack of fit by the deviance. The null deviance \(D_0\) compares the intercept-only model with the saturated model. The residual deviance \(D\) compares the fitted model with the saturated model. Maximising the log-likelihood is the same operation as making \(D\) small.

For nested models the likelihood-ratio statistic is the drop in deviance,

\[ D_{\text{small}} - D_{\text{large}} \sim \chi^2, \]

with degrees of freedom equal to the number of extra parameters. In R that is anova(model1, model2, test = "Chisq"). This is the GLM form of the analysis of deviance.

Their pseudo-\(R^2\) is \(1 - D/D_0\). They say that several pseudo-\(R^2\) exist, and that a nested comparison should be the likelihood-ratio test. AIC is the comparison they allow when the models are not nested: deviance, plus a penalty for the number of parameters.

Equidispersion is one parameter doing two jobs

StatHarbor Analytics, Count Data Models: From Poisson to Zero-Inflated Models, starts from \(Y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)\) with \(\mathbb{E}(Y) = \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mu\). One rate controls the centre and the spread. The log link makes a one-unit step in \(x_j\) multiply \(\mu\) by \(e^{\beta_j}\), a rate ratio.

The Poisson fit is the one they keep when the conditional mean is close to the conditional variance, the zeros are no more numerous than the Poisson predicts, and events are independent inside a fixed exposure. When \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) > \mathbb{E}(Y)\), the same fit understates the standard errors. They name unobserved heterogeneity, omitted covariates, and clustering as the usual sources.

A gamma latent rate is a negative binomial

The same notes derive the negative binomial as a Poisson–gamma compound: the Poisson rate is itself a gamma draw, and that draw is integrated out. The extra variance is unobserved heterogeneity.

\[ \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{\alpha} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x) = \mu + \alpha\mu^2. \]

McCullagh and Nelder use the first form. The simulation slides call that shape \(\theta\). In the second form, \(\alpha \to 0\) returns the Poisson. Estimating \(\alpha\) leaves the one-parameter exponential family.

Treating \(\alpha\) as known keeps the model inside a one-parameter GLM. Estimating it is full maximum likelihood. StatHarbor reaches for the negative binomial when the variance exceeds the mean and the zeros come from the same process as the positive counts, and compares it with the Poisson by a likelihood-ratio test.

A point mass of zeros is a mixture

A zero-inflated model is a different generative story. With probability \(\pi_0\) the observation is a structural zero. With probability \(1-\pi_0\) it is a draw from a count distribution \(f\). StatHarbor writes \(\pi_0\) as \(p\).

\[ \Pr(Y=0) = \pi_0 + (1-\pi_0)\,f(0), \qquad \Pr(Y=k>0) = (1-\pi_0)\,f(k). \]

A logit for \(\pi_0\) plus a log-linear Poisson is the zero-inflated Poisson. The negative binomial in \(f\) is the zero-inflated negative binomial. Neither is an exponential-family GLM.

StatHarbor compares a zero-inflated model with an ordinary Poisson by AIC, BIC, or a Vuong test, because those two models are not nested.

The count measures a latent share

Sarkar and Stephens separate a measurement model from a model for the latent intensity. The paper is about genes in single cells. The same split applies to any feature counted inside a sample of finite depth: a taxon, a transcript, a fragment.

Let \(s_i\) be the library size, the total count in sample \(i\), and let \(\lambda_{i\ell}\) be the relative intensity of feature \(\ell\), a share of that sample. The measurement model is

\[ Y_{i\ell} \mid s_i, \lambda_{i\ell} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(s_i \lambda_{i\ell}). \]

Dividing by the depth is this rate: the offset \(\log s_i\), with its coefficient fixed at 1.

Three assumptions in the measurement layer

Box 1 of Sarkar and Stephens lists the assumptions behind that Poisson measurement model.

In each sample, each molecule is equally likely to be observed.

Each molecule is observed independently of the others.

Only a small proportion of the molecules present are observed. They cite 10–20% in a typical single-cell experiment.

Assumptions 2 and 3 are the ones they treat as plausible. Assumption 1 fails when stability, cellular location, or sequence changes the chance that a molecule is seen. A bias that is shared across samples can enter the Poisson model as a covariate. A bias that changes from sample to sample is extra noise, and the measurement model that carries it is negative binomial. If a later protocol observes a larger share of the molecules, the measurement variance falls relative to a Poisson.

A zero is an observation

Sarkar and Stephens refuse the phrase “missing data” for a zero count. Their picture: a minute with no cars at an intersection is a zero; an hour when the observer is away is a missing record. A sequencing zero is the first of those. Imputing only the zeros treats them as holes.

They also drop the word “dropout” for a supposed device that writes a zero whatever the true intensity. Spike-in controls, in the papers they cite, did not support a zero-inflated measurement model. A zero-inflated observation model can still be the right margin when the latent intensity itself has a point mass at zero.

The observation model is the margin

An expression model, in their words, is a distribution \(g_\ell\) for the latent share \(\lambda_{i\ell}\). The observation model is the distribution of \(Y\) after \(\lambda\) has been integrated out.

A gamma \(g_\ell\) produces a negative-binomial margin. A point-gamma \(g_\ell\), a spike at zero plus a gamma, produces a zero-inflated negative-binomial margin. The Poisson recommendation is for the measurement layer. The margin is usually wider, because the latent share varies across samples.

The first two moments, by the tower rule

Write the Poisson rate as a latent \(\lambda\) with \(\mathbb{E}(\lambda) = \mu\). The law of total expectation and the law of total variance give the margin without a new pmf:

\[ \mathbb{E}(Y) = \mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\lambda)\} = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}(\lambda) + \mathrm{Var}(\lambda) = \mu + \mathrm{Var}(\lambda). \]

Compound, mixture, and the Tweedie line

\(\lambda\) fixed: \(\mathrm{Var}(\lambda) = 0\), and \(Y\) is Poisson.

\(\lambda\) continuous: a compound distribution. A gamma \(\lambda\) is the negative binomial. A normal draw for \(\log\lambda\) is the Poisson log-normal. Townes notes that this margin has a heavier tail than the negative binomial.

The latent discrete: a mixture. The zero-inflated model is the spike at a structural zero plus a count distribution.

Townes’s Poisson–Tweedie has \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mu + \phi\mu^{p}\). The negative binomial is \(p = 2\). These margins generally have no closed likelihood.

Independent counts, given their sum

Townes’s identity: independent Poisson counts, conditioned on their total, are one multinomial draw. If \(Y_\ell \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_\ell)\) independently and \(M = \sum Y_\ell\), then

\[ \mathbf{Y} \mid M \sim \mathrm{Multinomial}\!\left(M,\; \pi_\ell = \lambda_\ell \big/ \textstyle\sum_k \lambda_k\right). \]

The same step with independent negative binomials that share a scale parameter returns a Dirichlet-multinomial. The shared scale is required. Negative binomials with a shared shape and different means do not, after conditioning, stay Dirichlet-multinomial.

A sequencing library is this conditioning. The Sarkar rate \(s_i\lambda_{i\ell}\) is the Poisson layer. Given the library size, the vector of features is multinomial. Modelling the counts as independent Poissons with an offset, or as one multinomial, are the two sides of that identity.

The regression is for \(\pi\)

Olszewski places logistic regression in the generalised linear model: the target is a function of \(\mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\). For a Bernoulli response that function is \(\mathrm{logit}(\pi)\), and \(\pi\) is the conditional mean. A classifier that thresholds \(\pi\) is one use of the fitted probability.

He dates the logit to Berkson, as an alternative to the probit, and the unified GLM to Nelder and Wedderburn (1972, their paper). In R the same statement is glm(y ~ x, family = binomial(link = "logit")).

A test of two proportions is this model

In the first of the two follow-ups, the Wald statistic for a difference of two proportions, with unpooled variances, matches the average marginal effect of a logistic regression with one binary predictor. The sampling scheme is two independent binomials, without a small-sample correction. He calls the algebra a conceptual bridge: the statistics match, and the note does not re-prove the asymptotic distribution.

The second note matches the pooled two-proportion \(z\), the statistic behind prop.test(), to the Rao score test of the same logistic model. The fitting criterion is cross-entropy

Denis Burakov, in the chapter embedded below, starts from Fisher’s binomial likelihood and writes the average negative log-likelihood. For \(y_i \in \{0,1\}\) and fitted probabilities \(\hat\pi_i\),

\[ -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i\log\hat\pi_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat\pi_i)\right]. \]

The factor \(1/n\) does not move the maximiser. This is the log loss, also called cross-entropy. In his table, \(y = 1\) and \(\hat\pi = 0.19\) costs \(-\log 0.19 = 1.66\); \(\hat\pi = 0.79\) costs \(0.23\). With an intercept in the model, the fitted probabilities add up to the number of observed events.

The logit, rather than the probit

Burakov quotes Berkson’s reasons for the logistic curve over the probit: a closed-form derivative, fits that on Berkson’s data were as close as the probit or closer, and coefficients that exponentiate to odds ratios. A probit coefficient is a change in a standard normal deviate.

Angelino and Marcone add the complementary log-log link, \(\log\{-\log(1-\pi)\} = \mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\), for a curve that is asymmetric about \(1/2\). They report that probit and complementary log-log fits are often close to the logit. The link on these slides stays the logit, so that \(\beta_1\) remains a change in log odds.

One-hot coding is a contrast with a reference

Burakov’s worked table has 700 borrowers, 100 defaults. High delinquency: 80 defaults out of 180. Low delinquency: 20 out of 520. The model is \(\mathrm{logit}(\pi) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\), with \(x = 1\) for high delinquency.

The intercept \(\hat\beta_0 = -3.219\) is the log-odds in the reference group, a probability of \(0.0385\). The slope \(\hat\beta_1 = 2.996\) multiplies the odds by \(e^{2.996} = 20\). One category is absorbed into the intercept. The coefficient is the contrast with that category.

Weight of evidence centres on the sample

Weight of evidence asks a different contrast with the same fit. The sample default rate is \(100/700 = 0.1429\), and its logit is \(-1.7918\). A category’s weight of evidence is its own log-odds minus that overall logit.

The difference of the two weights equals the one-hot slope: \(\Delta\mathrm{WOE} = 2.9957 = \hat\beta_1\). Subtracting a constant does not change a variance, so the standard error of a weight of evidence equals the standard error of that category’s log-odds.

With a single categorical predictor the two codings describe one model. With several categorical predictors, Burakov notes that one-hot coefficients move when the reference level changes, while the weights of evidence stay centred on the overall event rate.

Softmax is the logit for \(K\) classes

For \(K\) mutually exclusive classes, \(\sum_k \pi_k = 1\), Burakov writes the softmax

\[ \Pr(Y = k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_j)}. \]

Fixing \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_K = \mathbf{0}\) leaves \(K-1\) vectors. Estimating all \(K\) is the unconstrained softmax. The probabilities match. \(K = 2\) with a zero reference class is binary logistic regression.

Softmax, drawn

The class probabilities sum to 1. The cross-entropy is \(-\sum_i\sum_k t_{ik}\log\pi_{ik}\), with \(t_{ik}\) the 1-of-\(K\) indicator.

Ordered categories are a cumulative model. Burakov notes them and stays with the unordered softmax.

A Wald interval is a large-sample interval for \(\pi\)

Burakov builds the interval for a new covariate row \(\mathbf{x}_0\) on the logit, then inverts it. The covariance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is \((\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\), with \(w_i = \hat\pi_i(1-\hat\pi_i)\).

\[ \mathbf{x}_0^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\mathbf{x}_0^{\top}\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\,\mathbf{x}_0}. \]

The endpoints are symmetric on the logit. After the inverse logit they sit inside \((0,1)\) and they are not symmetric on the probability scale. A conformal interval uses a calibration set

The same chapter then builds an interval whose coverage is finite-sample, under exchangeability, from a calibration set that was not used to fit \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

Fit the logit on the training rows.

On the calibration rows, score each residual. The Pearson form he uses is \((y - \hat\pi)/\sqrt{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)}\).

Add and subtract a quantile of those scores. On the logit scale the interval is symmetric around \(\mathrm{logit}(\hat\pi)\), and the inverse logit brings it back to \((0,1)\) without clipping.

A classical conformal prediction set for a binary label is a set of classes. The interval here is for \(\pi\). Whether the 0/1 outcome falls inside a narrow band for \(\pi\) is a different question, and he treats it as the wrong coverage check.

Ad hoc, post hoc, intrinsic

Qin Ma uses the three words for bioinformatics, and they transfer. An ad hoc analysis answers one question with one pre-chosen tool. A post hoc analysis, in that paper, reconciles several tools or several datasets after the fact. An intrinsic analysis builds the check into the model.

In this workshop “post hoc” also means the comparisons you make after a model that has more than two levels. Those are not the same operation. The next slides are the second meaning. Choosing the comparison because it was the significant one is data dredging: the hypothesis was suggested by the same data that are asked to confirm it.

Contrasts from the model you fitted

When the groups and the covariates are in one model, the comparisons are contrasts from that fit. Heiss separates two averages. An average marginal effect averages the contrast over the observed rows. A marginal effect at the mean evaluates it at the average covariate. marginaleffects defaults to the first. emmeans defaults to the second. They differ when the model is not linear.

Reporting several of those contrasts is still a family. The adjustment is simultaneous inference for the family you planned, not a correction chosen after the significant rows appear.

A row of coefficients is a family

\[ H_{0j}: \beta_j = 0, \quad j = 1, \ldots, p. \]

One row per coefficient hides that family. The matching statement is a joint region, or simultaneous intervals: Bonferroni intervals for a Bonferroni test, Tukey or Scheffé intervals for those contrast families. A logistic, Poisson, or negative-binomial model has the same family. The criterion does not change because the distribution did.

Genomics is a large exploratory family

A screen of thousands of genes, tens of thousands of proteins, or millions of variants is a different family. Missing a true signal is costly, and a controlled share of false leads is acceptable.

That is a false-discovery rate: Benjamini–Hochberg, an adaptive version, or a (q)-value. A (q)-value is not a posterior probability that the finding is true. A family-wise guarantee of no false claim at all spends power the screen cannot afford.

One false claim, or some false signal

A family-wise error rate is the right target when even one false claim is costly.

A false-discovery rate is the right target when the aim is broad discovery and some controlled false signal is acceptable.

Dependence, a hierarchy, or a structured set of contrasts often recovers power that a generic worst-case correction spends.

A procedure that controls the family-wise rate also controls the false-discovery rate, usually at that cost in power.

Independence, drawn

The fitted counts are row total times column total over \(n\), and every odds ratio equals 1. A logit model is the conditional distribution of one margin. The moments sit above the Poisson line

Aitchison and Ho, as quoted by Chiquet, Mariadassou, and Robin, give the margin after integrating the latent:

\[ \mathbb{E}(Y_{i\ell}) = \exp\!\left(o_{i\ell} + m_{i\ell} + \sigma_{\ell\ell}/2\right), \]

\[ \mathrm{Var}(Y_{i\ell}) = \mathbb{E}(Y_{i\ell}) + \mathbb{E}(Y_{i\ell})^2\left(e^{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}-1\right). \]

The variance exceeds the mean. The covariance of two features has the sign of \(\sigma_{\ell\ell'}\). Given \(\mathbf{Z}_i\), the features are independent, so the dependence lives in \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). Twice the effort adds \(\log 2\) to the offset \(o_{i\ell}\).

The likelihood has no closed form once \(\mathbf{Z}\) is integrated out. The paper fits it by a variational approximation, in the R package PLNmodels. Smooth \(\mu(x)\), and say what you assumed

StatHarbor Analytics, Smoothing and Nonparametric Methods, uses “nonparametric” for a curve that is not fixed in advance as a short formula. Complexity may grow with \(n\). The structure that replaces the formula is smoothness, locality, or a penalty.

Assumption

\[ Y = \mu(x) + \varepsilon, \qquad \mathbb{E}(\varepsilon \mid x) = 0. \] Their \(f(x)\) is this \(\mu(x)\). A straight line \(\mu(x) = \mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is efficient when it is close to the curve, and biased when the curve bends.

Angelino and Marcone make the same opening: a linear model is easy to interpret, and the straight line is often an approximation.

One path, rising flexibility

Left to right: a straight mean, a local polynomial, a bandwidth chosen by GCV, a spline with knots, a spline with a penalty, then one smooth per covariate.

Each step spends a tuning choice: a bandwidth, a set of knots, or a penalty. The last step is how that choice survives more than one covariate.

Flexibility spends variance

At a point \(x_0\), StatHarbor decomposes the error in the curve:

\[ \mathbb{E}\big(\hat\mu(x_0) - \mu(x_0)\big)^2 = \mathrm{Var}\big(\hat\mu(x_0)\big) + \mathrm{Bias}\big(\hat\mu(x_0)\big)^2. \]

Predicting a new \(Y\) adds \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon \mid x_0)\) on top of that. More flexibility usually cuts the bias and raises the variance. A small bandwidth is the noisy end. A large bandwidth is the oversmoothed end.

A kernel is a local bump

Before the regression, the same idea estimates a density. Each observation contributes a bump of width \(h\):

\[ \hat f_h(x) = \frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n} K\!\left(\frac{x_i - x}{h}\right). \]

They name four kernels: Gaussian, Epanechnikov, uniform, and triangular. Bandwidth matters far more than which of those four you pick.

For a Gaussian kernel, Silverman’s rule of thumb is \(\hat h \approx 1.06\,\hat\sigma\, n^{-1/5}\). They say it is often adequate for a roughly unimodal symmetric density, and that it fails for a skewed, multimodal, or heavy-tailed density. The AMISE-optimal bandwidth shrinks as \(n^{-1/5}\): nonparametric rates are slower than a well-specified parametric rate.

Local weights for \(\mu(x_0)\)

Model

\[ \hat\mu(x_0) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i(x_0)\, Y_i, \qquad \sum_i w_i(x_0) = 1. \] Nadaraya–Watson sets \(w_i\) from \(K((x_i-x_0)/h)\). That fit is a local constant: a weighted average of nearby \(Y_i\).

Near the edge of \(x\) the window is one-sided, so a local constant is pulled inward and the curve flattens. That boundary bias is why the next fit is local linear.

Fit a line in the window

At \(x_0\), minimise the weighted residual sum of squares of a line in \((x_i - x_0)\). The fitted value is the local intercept.

Degree 0 is the local constant, Nadaraya–Watson.

Degree 1 is local linear. StatHarbor’s default: less boundary bias, still local.

Degree 2 is local quadratic.

Angelino and Marcone’s LOESS often uses the quadratic. The two sources agree on the algorithm and differ on the default degree.

LOESS is that regression, with a span

Model

LOESS is local polynomial regression with a nearest-neighbour window. The span is a proportion of the sample, in \((0, 1]\). StatHarbor’s example is \(0.3\). The window widens where \(x\) is sparse. The usual weight is the tricube kernel.

Angelino and Marcone: choose the span, find the nearest neighbours, weight them, fit the local polynomial.

The fit does not start from a named formula, and it does not return one.

It wants a large, densely sampled \(x\). Their use is exploratory smoothing of a scatterplot.

GCV chooses the bandwidth

A linear smoother is \(\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{Y}\). Leave-one-out cross-validation refits \(n\) times. Generalised cross-validation approximates it:

\[ \mathrm{GCV}(h) = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i\big(Y_i - \hat\mu(x_i)\big)^2}{\big(1 - \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S})/n\big)^2}. \]

The bandwidth that minimises GCV is their predictive choice. The same criterion, with the penalty weight in place of \(h\), is how they tune a smoothing spline. Kernel choice stays secondary.

A global polynomial moves the whole curve

Angelino and Marcone extend the line by powers of \(x\). A quadratic uses \(x\) and \(x^2\). They stop at degree 3 or 4: higher powers become strange near the ends of \(x\). StatHarbor’s matching warning is that a local change then alters the fit far away.

Lopez-Ayala, Riley, Collins, and Zimmermann add fractional polynomials: powers that need not be whole numbers. The function is still defined on the whole range of \(x\), so a pile of values in one tail can distort the shape everywhere.

A regression spline spends its flexibility at knots

Model

Knots \(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_K\) cut \(x\) into intervals. A cubic piece is fitted in each, with \(f\), \(f'\), and \(f''\) continuous at the knots. The truncated-power basis is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \sum_k \theta_k (x-\xi_k)_+^3\).

The analyst chooses how many knots, and where. A common default is the quantiles of \(x\). Angelino and Marcone: after the continuity constraints, each added knot costs about one degree of freedom, and the spline is most flexible where the knots are dense. A step function, their piecewise constant, misses the trend unless the cut-points are real breaks. That is the dichotomisation case.

B-splines span the same curves

The truncated-power basis is clear and numerically unstable. A B-spline basis spans the same spline space with local support: each basis function is zero outside a few intervals. The design matrix is sparse, and the fit is better conditioned. Software prefers this basis. The knots are still chosen by hand. The next smoother removes that choice and penalises roughness instead.

A penalty replaces the knot count

Model

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(Y_i - \mu(x_i)\big)^2 + \lambda \int \big(\mu''(t)\big)^2\,\mathrm{d}t. \] The first term is fidelity. The integral is curvature. Here \(\lambda\) is the penalty weight, not a Poisson rate and not a relative intensity. Large \(\lambda\) forces a smooth curve. Small \(\lambda\) allows a wiggly one. A straight line has \(\mu'' = 0\), so the penalty does not forbid a bend. It discourages a bend the data do not support.

The solution is a natural cubic spline

Theorem

The minimiser, over a suitable class of functions, is a natural cubic spline with a knot at every observed \(x_i\).

Angelino and Marcone list the same properties: piecewise cubic, knots at the distinct \(x_i\), continuous first and second derivatives, and linear beyond the extreme knots. An infinite-dimensional penalty problem becomes a finite spline. In matrix form it is penalised least squares, the ridge pattern \((\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{N} + \lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\).

Effective degrees of freedom are \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_\lambda)\). As \(\lambda \to \infty\) the fit approaches a straight line. As \(\lambda \to 0\) it approaches interpolation. GCV, with \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_\lambda)\) in the denominator, chooses \(\lambda\).

Restricted cubics are the applied form of that constraint

The BMJ guide calls the natural spline a restricted cubic spline: cubics between the inner knots, and a straight line before the first knot and after the last. The tails, where points are few and outliers bite, stay stable. With \(k\) knots the model spends \(k-1\) degrees of freedom. Three knots, their usual start, sit at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of \(x\). Three to five knots is the range they recommend, and AIC can choose the number. The exact quantile locations rarely change the curve.

Coefficients of a spline are not the summary. The partial-effect plot is.

The curve can rise twice

On the meningitis data, CSF glucose against bacterial meningitis: a straight line, and a cut at 62 mg/dL, both miss the second rise at high glucose that the three-knot restricted cubic shows.

Assumption

Do not categorise a continuous covariate, and do not assume a straight line, whether the aim is to predict, explain, or describe. Spend degrees of freedom first on the covariates that matter. Choosing the shape by eye, before the fit, spends phantom degrees of freedom and makes the interval too narrow.

A neighbourhood empties as dimension grows

Local methods need nearby observations. As the number of covariates grows, those neighbourhoods empty, and “local” stops meaning a small window. Naive multivariate smoothing breaks down. The practical compromise is an additive mean: one smooth per covariate, estimated as a one-dimensional problem.

A GAM is an additive smooth on the link

Model

\[ g(\mu) = \beta_0 + s_1(x_1) + \cdots + s_p(x_p), \qquad \mu = \mathbb{E}(Y \mid \mathbf{x}). \] A logistic GAM uses the logit. A Poisson GAM uses the log. Each \(s_j\) is a one-dimensional smoother. Backfitting cycles through partial residuals until the smooths settle.

Because the mean is a sum, each \(\hat s_j\) has its own plot: a line, a threshold, a saturation, a U-shape. StatHarbor’s distinction: that plot is the component, not a black-box importance score. The BMJ guide names mgcv for this fit, beside rms for the restricted cubic, and treats GAMs and LOESS as the alternatives to fractional polynomials and splines.

Exploratory visualisation: LOESS.

Experimental design

🧮 Twenty minutes for the plan. Vocabulary, contrasts, and the longitudinal close are the reference stack.

The analysis inherits the plan

A mixed model does not repair an undescribed design. Before the formula, name:

  • the unit that received the treatment
  • the unit that was measured
  • what was randomised, blocked, or only observed
  • which rows are repeats of the same unit

Carbon data are often hierarchical, multi-site, and repeated in time. The random effect is the translation of that structure, not a decorative term.

A short map of plans

Plan What is randomised or assigned
Completely randomised Treatments to units, with no blocking factor
Randomised block Treatments inside a block that soaks up a nuisance gradient
Factorial Two or more factors crossed, so that interactions exist

Tanaka’s edibble slides are a precise way to write units and treatments down before you fit anything.

Completely randomised, then blocked

Completely randomised: every enclosure could have received every warming level, and you trust the site to be homogeneous.

Blocked: you group units that share a nuisance (a boardwalk, a water-table band, a day) and randomise inside the block.

SPRUCE’s design page says a block was considered, and that preliminary surveys did not show a gradient strong enough to require one. That is a design assumption, and it belongs in the report.

Factorial: the interaction is the point

Warming and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) are crossed. The scientific question on the design page is not only “does warming matter?” It is also “does elevated \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) change the warming response?”

That second question is \(\beta_3\) in

\[ g(\mu_{ij}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1j} + \beta_2 x_{2j} + \beta_3 x_{1j} x_{2j} + \cdots \]

If you fit two separate slopes and then compare their \(p\)-values, you have skipped \(\beta_3\).

Split-plot: the large unit is hard to change

A factor that is expensive to reset is applied to the large unit. A factor that is easy to reset is applied inside it.

Cookie-dough batches and oven trays are the textbook version. In the field, an enclosure’s \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) setpoint and a collar’s measurement schedule are not interchangeable units.

The error term for the large-unit factor is the large-unit variance. Using the inner residual will make the interval too small.

Crossed, nested, or both

Warming and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) are crossed on the enclosure. Collars and dates are nested in the enclosure. A technical replicate nested in a core is a further level, and it is rarely the level of inference.

A random intercept is a distribution on \(u_j\)

Use a Gaussian equation for \(Y_{ij}\) only when \(Y\) can live on that scale. For a count or a detection, the cluster term sits on the link, as in the diagram.

\(u_j\) is a property of the cluster. It is not a covariate you forgot to measure, unless you are willing to say so. Ignoring it is the pitfall repeated rows are not replicates.

Fixed and random name the target

  • Fixed: a contrast you want to report, such as the warming slope
  • Random: a level you want to generalise over, such as enclosures drawn from a bog
  • If you have the whole population of levels, a fixed indicator set is coherent
  • Ten enclosures is a small sample of a variance. \(\hat\sigma_u\) will be noisy

Partial pooling is the practical reason to bother: an enclosure with two bad weeks borrows strength from the others. The meta-analysis version, one estimate per site, is fixed effect or random effects.

Repeated measures are a time series inside \(j\)

Do not run a separate test at each date and then narrate the dates that were “significant”.

A first model for a repeated flux:

  • a term for time, or for season
  • the treatment, which does not change inside the enclosure
  • the treatment \(\times\) time interaction, if the contrast itself changes
  • a dependence for the residuals, or a random intercept for the enclosure

Dates are \(i\). They do not become new experimental units because there are many of them.

SPRUCE chose a regression, not five bins

The design page records an earlier incomplete factorial, then a quantitative comparison: more temperature levels, used as a regression, resolved the shape better than a small ANOVA, at lower cost.

Do not bin the gradient you built

The design page allows a fallback: bin the ten enclosures into low, medium, and high for an ANOVA, if a variable forces it.

That fallback spends the design. Adjacent levels, \(+4.5\) and \(+6.75\), become either “the same” or “different” by a cut. The curve we already plotted is exactly what the binning throws away.

Keep \(x_1\) continuous. Report the slope per \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\), with an interval, and show the fitted mean. The same cut, as a reporting pitfall, is do not dichotomise.

A controlled experiment sets the conditions

Huber, chapter 13: you control the system, the environment, and the readout.

His vignette is a characterised cell line, a defined medium, a measured dose, and a pathway reporter read at 72 h.

A study leaves some conditions free

In a study, conditions that move the outcome are outside the investigator’s control. Huber’s field example is the weather, the food, and the predators.

In an observational study the variable of interest itself is not assigned. That is where his reminder sits: correlation is not causation.

A trial randomises the assignment

A randomised controlled trial still cannot hold every nuisance fixed. It does control who receives the intervention.

With a large enough sample, the nuisances average out, and the contrast can be read as an effect of that assignment.

The trial is prospective: the outcome is unknown when the groups are assigned.

A meta-analysis studies the studies

Huber calls a meta-analysis an observational study of earlier experiments or studies. Two motives: a larger effective sample, and the hope that study-level bias averages out across studies.

The hierarchical version, one estimate per site, is fixed effect or random effects.

Twenty weighings are not ten people

Huber’s weight-loss question. Twenty weighings of one person, on a precise scale, remeasure a value you already know. Ten people, weighed once each, start to ask whether the effect travels.

Name the level you can generalise to: laboratory, operator, machine, protocol, strain, litter, animal. “Technical” and “biological” are too coarse for that list.

Randomise the nuisance you cannot block

When the nuisance is unknown in advance, randomisation is Huber’s practical strategy: at a large enough sample, its effect averages out. It also limits unconscious bias, such as extra care for the samples that were hard to obtain.

He points to Senn (2004) for the ways a “better” scheme than simple randomisation can go wrong.

Balance makes each effect identifiable

A balanced design gives every factor combination the same number of replicates. Each factor’s effect can then be separated. A nuisance that is balanced against the factor of interest is in the same position.

An unbalanced plan can be forced by cost. Huber’s warning is that the analysis is then on thin ice.

Pairing is the smallest block

Block what you can, and randomise what you cannot. A paired design is the simplest block: twins, or the same person before and after.

Fisher criticised Darwin for putting every cross-pollinated plant on the same side of the pot. A coin flip for the side would have kept that side from masquerading as the cross.

When a natural pair does not exist, a matched design builds pairs on age, sex, or health, and treats one of the pair.

Treatment contrasts use one reference

Larmarange, chapter 26. For an unordered factor, R’s default is contr.treatment. A factor with \(n\) levels becomes \(n-1\) columns. The reference level sits in the intercept.

In his marker ~ grade fit the intercept is \(1.0669\) and the grade II coefficient is \(-0.3864\). The mean at grade II is the sum:

\[ 1.0669 + (-0.3864) = 0.6805. \]

contr.treatment(n, base =) and contr.SAS() move which level is the reference.

Sum contrasts use the mean of the level means

contr.sum compares each level with the unweighted mean of the level means. The last level is coded \(-1\), so one factor’s coefficients sum to zero and the intercept is that grand mean.

The grade level that was coded \(-1\) has deviation \(-(0.1525 - 0.2339) = 0.0814\), which is also \(0.9958 - 0.9144\).

Fitted values, residual sum of squares, and AIC match the treatment-contrast fit. What changes is the reading of each coefficient.

An ordered factor can carry a trend

Further codings in the same chapter.

MASS::contr.sdif compares each level with the one before it. For grade, II minus I is \(-0.39\), the same gap as the treatment contrast against grade I.

contr.helmert compares each new level with the mean of the earlier levels. Keep it for a question that is exactly that comparison.

contr.poly is the default for an ordered factor. The columns are a linear, a quadratic, and a cubic trend. That is the coding for ordered time or dose when the question is the shape, including a bend. The intercept is again the mean of the level means.

Choose the runs before the measurements

A design of experiments picks the combinations you will run, before any \(Y\) is recorded. The organising question is the objective, not the historical name of the matrix.

A smaller matrix buys its size by giving something up. The usual sacrifice is aliasing: two effects share one column, so the fit cannot tell them apart. The four families below are the ones the overview treats as the working set: fractional factorials, definitive screening, a response surface, and an optimal design.

NIST’s selection chapter is the map: how do you select an experimental design?

A fraction spends the interactions

A full factorial of \(k\) two-level factors needs \(2^k\) runs. Ten factors need \(1024\). A fractional factorial keeps \(2^{k-p}\) runs. Five factors at a quarter fraction need \(2^{5-2} = 8\), against \(32\) for the full \(2^5\).

Resolution says which effects are aliased. A low resolution is a screen for main effects. A high resolution leaves the interactions you care about separable. NIST’s note on resolution is here.

A definitive screen also sees a curve

Plackett–Burman is the classical screen: \(11\) two-level factors in \(12\) runs, with main effects aliased with two-factor interactions. NIST describes it as a design for when the main effects are the focus.

A definitive screening design is the modern alternative for continuous factors. The run count is about \(2k+1\), so \(14\) factors need about \(29\) runs rather than \(2^{14}\). Each factor has levels \(-1\), \(0\), and \(+1\), so a quadratic term \(\beta_{11} x^2\) can be seen. JMP’s account: main effects are orthogonal to two-factor interactions, and those interactions are not completely aliased with each other. Definitive screening designs.

A surface needs three levels

Once a few factors remain, the question moves from “which factor?” to “where is the optimum?”. The working model is quadratic: a linear term, a square, and a product for each pair.

A central composite design is a factorial, plus axial points \((\pm\alpha, 0)\) and \((0, \pm\alpha)\), plus centre points.

A Box–Behnken design also estimates that quadratic surface, and it avoids the corner where every factor is at its extreme.

Both are in JMP’s introduction to DOE.

An optimal design is computed for the constraint

D-optimal, I-optimal, and A-optimal designs are algorithm-built matrices. They are the modern choice when the run budget is tight, the factors are a mix of continuous and categorical, or the experimental region is an awkward shape.

The matrix is less readable than a textbook factorial. The criterion is explicit: D-optimality, for example, targets the precision of the coefficient estimates. A mixture, where the factors are parts of a constant sum, needs its own geometry rather than one of these factorials.

A decision tree for the matrix

  • Objective, chosen before any measurement
    • Screen, when many factors might matter: a fractional factorial, Plackett–Burman, or a definitive screening design
      • Interactions, once a few factors remain: a full or high-resolution factorial
        • Optimise: a central composite, a Box–Behnken, or an optimal design
    • Taguchi, when the target is robustness to uncontrolled noise
    • Mixture, when the factors are parts of a constant sum

Nesting is a property of the experiment

Schielzeth and Nakagawa (Methods in Ecology and Evolution, 2013). A nested factor can be fitted with the syntax of a crossed factor, provided each nested level has a unique label. The design still decides what the variances mean.

The difference that matters is the interaction variance. In a nested experiment it is pooled with the main-effect variance of the nested factor. A crossed experiment can separate the two. The pooling is fixed by which combinations occur, not by the formula.

If a cross would have been feasible and meaningful, the data are nested by design, and the pooling has to be stated. If a cross would be irrelevant, the nesting is natural.

Repeated in time is longitudinal

Angelino and Marcone, September 2026: a repeated-measures design records each individual more than once. The design is longitudinal when those repeats are ordered in time.

Three predictors sit beside \(Y\): time, a covariate that changes with time, and a covariate that does not. The aim is how \(\mu\) depends on them across the waves.

Godechot’s panel is the econometric name for the same layout: several dates for one person, set against a single cross-section. Within-person changes become visible, and age can be separated from cohort.

Classical repeated-measures ANOVA wants a rectangle

The same course lists two limits. The ANOVA keeps only people with every wave. It also assumes sphericity: every pair of times has the same variance of the difference. When that fails, the \(F\) ratios inflate.

A mixed model keeps an incomplete person. The dependence goes into \(u_j\), or into a residual correlation. Dates stay index \(i\). They do not become new experimental units.

Fixed levels are the ones you would use again

Angelino and Marcone: a fixed factor has levels you chose in order to compare them, and you would use those levels again. Sex, in their example, is fixed. It shifts the mean of \(Y\).

A random factor has levels you can treat as a sample from a population of effects. They need not reappear. The target is their variance, and the usual model for that draw is Gaussian. The same factor can be either, depending on the question. The earlier statement is fixed and random name the target.

Godechot’s condition for a random person effect \(a_i\): it is uncorrelated with the covariates. When it is not, he centres each person, the within estimator, and treats \(a_i\) as fixed. His Hausman rule follows the comparison: if the within fit and the random-effect fit disagree, keep the within fit.

The line is allowed to vary inside \(j\)

Model

\[ Y_{ij} = (\beta_0 + u_{0j}) + (\beta_1 + u_{1j})\, t_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}. \]

\(j\) is the person, the child, or the enclosure. \(i\) is the wave. \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are the mean intercept and the mean slope. \(u_{0j}\) and \(u_{1j}\) are the cluster’s departures, mean zero, usually Gaussian.

Angelino and Marcone’s sketch replaces a separate line for each of sixteen children with these four parameters. Ignoring \(j\) treats the waves as independent, and the interval uses the wrong variance. The same hierarchy is what lets a longitudinal sample be a multilevel model.

Bayesian fits and causal estimands

⚖️ Twenty minutes for the probability statement. Simpson, the DAG roles, and the network search are the reference stack.

The mean can stay; the probability statement changes

A Bayesian fit of the same GLM still uses

\[ g(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i \]

and a family for \(Y \mid x\). What changes is the status of \(\beta\).

  • the likelihood is the model for \(Y\) given \(\beta\)
  • the prior is what you are willing to say about \(\beta\) before these rows
  • the posterior is the updated distribution of \(\beta\) given the rows

Riley and colleagues note one use directly: a probability such as \(\Pr(\text{rate ratio} < 0.9 \mid \text{data})\) (Riley et al. 2022).

Three objects, in order

No prior is “non-informative” merely because it was the software default. A prior labelled vague can still move \(\hat\beta\) when the sample is small or the outcome is sparse. That check is a sensitivity analysis.

What you are allowed to say

A Wald interval from glm is a statement about the sampling distribution of the estimator, under the model, if the experiment were repeated.

A posterior interval is a statement about \(\beta\) given this sample and this prior.

They can be numerically close for a large sample and a gentle prior. They are not the same sentence. Write the one you computed.

With ten enclosures, the prior on \(\sigma_u\) is not a detail. Refit with a second prior and show the slope again.

Where it earns its place this afternoon

  • partial pooling of sites or enclosures, with an honest prior on the variance
  • a parameter that must stay positive, such as a variance or a rate
  • a small sample, where a weakly identified \(\beta\) should not look precise
  • a hierarchical summary across studies, which is the meta-analysis version of the same idea

It does not remove confounding, and it does not choose the estimand.

A coefficient is not an intervention

SPRUCE assigns warming and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\). A slope on assigned warming estimates the effect of that assignment, in that bog, under the design assumptions.

An observational regression of taxon counts on organic carbon does not estimate “the effect of setting carbon to a new value”, unless the adjustment set matches the causal question.

Randomisation is a design fact. Adding covariates is not a substitute for it.

Two hospitals, one survival rate

François Giltaire, Le paradoxe de Simpson. A public-health report prints one number per hospital. Survival is 86% at A and 79% at B. The booking that follows from those two numbers is A.

Inside each severity, B is higher

A takes mostly light cases. B takes mostly severe cases. Each hospital has 1000 patients.

Light cases Severe cases Overall
A 90% of 800 70% of 200 86%
B 95% of 200 75% of 800 79%

Light cases: B is above A. Severe cases: B is above A. The single published rate still ranks A above B.

The overall rate is a weighted mean

The overall rate is the subgroup rates weighted by how many patients sit in each subgroup.

\[ \frac{800 \times 0.9 + 200 \times 0.7}{800 + 200} = 86\% \]

\[ \frac{200 \times 0.95 + 800 \times 0.75}{200 + 800} = 79\% \]

The denominators match. A puts a weight of \(n = 800\) on the light cases, where the rates are high. B puts a weight of \(n = 200\) on those same cases. Giltaire’s point: that difference in weights is enough to reverse the overall ranking.

Severity mixes two hospitals that are not the same

Severity changes which hospital the patients go to, and it changes the survival rate. Pooling the light cases with the severe cases compares hospitals that did not treat the same mix.

Giltaire’s statement of the paradox: a trend that holds in every subgroup can reverse once the subgroups are pooled, because the subgroups do not have the same proportions. The soil version of this arrow pattern is a confounder in the soil table.

A confounder in the soil table

Both carbon and the taxon respond to moisture. The unadjusted slope of \(Y\) on carbon mixes the carbon path with the moisture path.

If the estimand is the association at a given moisture, moisture belongs in the model. If moisture is how carbon affects the taxon, moisture is a mediator, and adjusting for it removes part of the effect you wanted. The pitfalls drawing is three arrows, three estimands.

A mediator is a different question

The top path is indirect. The lower path is direct. The total effect of assigned warming leaves plant productivity alone. Conditioning on productivity estimates something else, and only under further assumptions.

Write the path you mean before you add the covariate.

The explanatory target is \(do(x)\)

Johannes Hertel, Linking statistics, computational biology and biochemistry. The question he sets is the effect of varying the independent variable on the response, in an observational study.

Lewis (1973), as Hertel quotes it: an event \(E\) depends causally on \(C\) when \(E\) would have occurred had \(C\) occurred, and would not have occurred had \(C\) not. Pearl (2000) writes the explanatory statement as \(P(\text{cancer} \mid do(\text{smoking}))\) and the statistical statement as \(P(\text{cancer} \mid \text{smoking})\).

Confounder, selection bias, and mediation are defined inside that framework. A prediction of \(Y\) from \(x\) does not use the \(do\).

One independence, two causal stories

Hertel: a directed acyclic graph is what lets you check a correlation pattern against a causal theory. One pattern can belong to more than one theory.

The shared statistical statement in his pair of examples: \(T\) is correlated with \(X\) and with \(Z\) whether or not you condition, and \(Z\) is conditionally independent of \(X\) given \(T\).

\(T\) is a common cause

\(T\) causes \(X\) and \(T\) causes \(Z\). There is no direct arrow between \(Z\) and \(X\). Conditioning on \(T\) leaves \(Z\) independent of \(X\).

\(Z\) acts through \(T\)

\(Z\) causes \(T\) and \(T\) causes \(X\). The conditional independence is the same as in the fork. The arrows are not settled by that independence.

The DAG is a set of assumptions

Hertel lists three, before the arrows can be read as causes.

Assumption

There is a DAG that is a causal representation of the system. A variable is independent of its non-effects given its direct causes. The only conditional independences in the data are the ones that DAG states.

The second sentence is his causal Markov condition. The third is faithfulness. He notes that a complete measurement of every variable in the graph is generally impossible, so the practical question is which variables the analysis has to include.

A third variable has a job

His names, for an observational effect of \(X\) on \(Y\). A confounder causes both. His example is age, between a disease phenotype and a metabolite. \(X\) and \(Y\) are then correlated even when they are unrelated. That is the soil-moisture diagram, a confounder in the soil table.

A mediator sits on the path from \(X\) to \(Y\). Controlling for it reduces, and can remove, the correlation. That is a mediator is a different question.

A variance factor touches only \(Y\)

\(Z\) causes \(Y\) and does not cause \(X\). Hertel’s example is a batch effect. \(Z\) does not create a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). It can mask one. He would control it, by design or in the model, to recover power.

An instrument touches only \(X\)

\(Z\) causes \(X\) and does not cause \(Y\). Hertel’s example is a genetic variant. \(Z\) does not create a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). It does not have to enter the regression. Some designs use it on purpose.

A collider is a common effect

\(X\) and \(Y\) both cause \(Z\). Hertel’s example is inflammation. Controlling for \(Z\) induces a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). A study restricted to healthy people is his selection-bias version of the same arrow: the restriction can create correlations that were not there.

He would not control a collider. His word for doing it anyway is overmatching. The Bayesian-network notes call this arrow a v-structure, and they connect it to selection bias.

A moderator changes the path

Hertel: \(Z\) changes the causal path from \(X\) to \(Y\). His example is sex. Women and men may show different metabolite profiles in response to a disease.

The analysis is then a stratum, or an interaction. Matching on \(Z\) does not do that job, and the sample has to be large enough for the interaction.

Adjust what the role requires

Hertel’s control list, for an explanatory analysis.

A confounder: control it, by matching or in the model, or the interpretation is wrong.

A collider: leave it out.

A variance factor: control it, for power.

A mediator: leave it out, unless the question is that path.

A moderator: an interaction or a stratum, not a match alone.

An instrument: optional. Some designs are built around it.

Which of these is age, and which is diet?

Hertel’s discussion, left as a discussion. Parkinson’s disease and the blood metabolome. The candidates he writes down: age, sex, BMI, kidney function, exercise, diet, smoking, Parkinson’s medication, and other medication.

The question he asks is which of those you would control, and which assumption that control uses. The six labels under his sketch are confounder, variance factor, collider, instrument, mediator, and moderator.

The simulated association matches only if the arrow is real

His last slide is a structural equation model on a DAG: host factors, a microbiome layer, community secretion, and the host metabolome. The sentence he draws from it:

“in silico association statistics” will correlate with “in vivo association statistics” if and only if the microbiome is causal for a metabolite in the host.

An association computed from the model tracks the association in the host when that causal arrow is the one in the diagram.

Sensitivity is the causal report

For an observational claim, say what would have to be true for the conclusion to change:

  • a missing confounder associated with both \(x\) and \(Y\) at a stated strength
  • a different adjustment set from the same diagram
  • a different family, if the mean is sensitive to the variance assumption
  • a different prior, if the fit is Bayesian and the sample is small

A direct link is not a direction

The Bayesian-network course notes separate two graphs. A dependency network joins two variables that still share a statistical relationship once the others are accounted for. That removes indirect co-variation. It does not say which way the link runs.

A regulatory direction needs a further claim, in the notes’ words: a change in \(X\) produces a change in \(Y\), and a change in \(Y\) does not change \(X\). That is the same gap as Hertel’s fork and chain, one independence, two causal stories.

Three graphs, one distribution

With \(Z\) between \(X\) and \(Y\), and no direct arrow from \(X\) to \(Y\), the notes count four directed acyclic graphs. Three of them encode the same conditional independences. Those three are one Markov equivalence class, drawn as a completed partially directed acyclic graph: an edge is directed only when the direction is identified, and left undirected when it is not.

Statistics on the observational distribution will not separate the three. The notes’ next step is a causal analysis.

A knock-out supplies the direction

When the graph was not drawn by an expert, the notes’ first source of direction is a perturbation. If knocking out \(X\) changes \(Y\), the notes write \(X \to Y\). They identify that experiment with the \(do\)-operator.

It is empirical, and it does not need a fitted network. It is a wet-lab result. Orienting a whole network this way needs many perturbations, which the notes say does not scale.

Time supplies the direction

The other source in the notes is a series. The regression is the dependency-network regression, moved forward one time step.

Model

\[ Y(t+1) \approx a_1 X_1(t) + \cdots + a_k X_k(t). \]

If \(a_i \ne 0\), then \(X_i\) regulates \(Y\) from one time to the next. The notes call this a dynamic Bayesian network: an initial state, the same transition from time to time, and the chain unrolled.

Tests, or a score, when nobody states the graph

The notes’ three searches, for the case with no expert graph.

Constraint-based: test a conditional independence. If the test rejects independence, the nodes are joined.

Score-based: give each candidate graph a score for how well it describes the data, and maximise the score.

Hybrid: learn the skeleton from the constraints, then refine it with a score.

Marco Scutari’s benchmark, as the notes report it, for the developer of bnlearn: at small samples the constraint-based algorithms were more accurate than the score-based ones, and as accurate as the hybrids. Tabu search, a score-based method, was faster than the constraint-based algorithms and faster than simulated annealing.

A searched graph is still a DAG

The notes’ limits on that search. The graph has no cycles, so a feedback loop or a self-regulation is outside it. Adding observations can change the direction of an edge. A network of thousands of nodes is too large to enumerate, and the search then needs a heuristic.

The two implementations they name are bnlearn in R and PyAgrum in Python.

Reproducibility

🔁 A reference stack. One source file, then the practices that keep a rerun honest.

Copying the result is the slow path

Jadey Ryan, Salt Lake City R Users Group, 1 July 2024. Data and style updates are inevitable. Doing them by hand is slow, and it is where the numbers drift.

Quarto renders the report and the slides

The same talk, the slide that replaces the copy. A data update or a data error changes the data source. A style update changes the tables, the plots, or the markdown. One render writes the report and the presentation.

Quarto is the publishing step

Ryan’s definition: a publishing system for more than one language and more than one output. The path she draws, from the Hello, Quarto keynote, is:

a .qmd file

knitr, or Jupyter

markdown, then Pandoc, then HTML, PDF, Word, or slides

The artwork on her slide is Allison Horst’s, for Julia Lowndes and Mine Çetinkaya-Rundel, rstudio::conf 2022.

One source, several outputs

From the same talk. A .qmd can be an article or a report, a manuscript, a presentation, a dashboard, a website, a book, or an interactive document.

The lists live in the format guide. The gallery is the set of finished examples. This deck is the presentation case.

Three parts, in this order

Ryan’s anatomy of a .qmd:

  1. a YAML header, for the metadata and the options
  2. the narrative, in markdown
  3. the code chunks, which import, wrangle, and draw

The header does not compute. The chunks do. The narrative is what a reader sees between them.

YAML is fussy about spaces

The header sits between two lines of three dashes. Each option is key: value. Each nested level is two spaces. Ryan’s reminder: YAML is fussy.

---
title: Quarto demo
format:
  html:
    toc: true
---

The option lists for HTML, PDF, Word, and reveal.js are the reference. This deck’s header is index.qmd.

Markdown is the narrative

Ryan’s short list. **bold** is bold. # and ## are the headings: in this deck, a level-1 heading is a section and a level-2 heading is a slide. [label](https://example.com) is a link. ![](image.png) is a figure. Inline code and inline maths use backticks.

The markdown guide is the rest of the syntax.

A chunk is the analysis, not a paste

A chunk opens with ```{r} and closes with ```. In RStudio, insert one with the toolbar button, or with Ctrl+Alt+I. Run the current chunk with Ctrl+Shift+Enter.

In this deck a chunk that would refit a model is marked eval: false, and the figure is a file written by scripts/generate-slide-figures.R. The number on the slide and the number in the file are then the same number.

A parameter is an input of the document

Ryan treats a parameterised report as a function. One .qmd, several reports, and the thing that changes is a value in params.

A parameter is a string, a number, or a Boolean.

A data frame has to be serialised to travel in as a parameter, then rebuilt inside the document. Her pointers are Dervieux and Helveston.

The default has to exist in the data

params:
  fave_breed: "Snowshoe"

Her name for the parameter is a column she will filter on. Any name is legal. If Snowshoe is not in the data, the code errors or the output is blank.

The same key is how you would swap a site, a year, or a warming level without a second copy of the file.

Read it with params$

params is a list. params$fave_breed is the string. In a sentence, the same call is inline R, written as a backtick, the letter r, and params$fave_breed.

In a plot title she uses paste(params$fave_breed, "population"), or glue::glue("{params$fave_breed} population"). Search the file for the hard-coded string and replace it with params$.

Three ways to render a variant

  1. The Render button, or Ctrl+Shift+K, uses the defaults in the header.
  2. From R, quarto::quarto_render() takes execute_params = list(fave_breed = "Snowshoe").
  3. From the terminal, quarto render template.qmd -P fave_breed:'Snowshoe'.

Her parameterised-reporting workshops are the step that renders every variant in one go. The source of this talk is jadeynryan/slc-rug-quarto.

Several formats, still one file

format:
  html:
    embed-resources: true
  pdf: default
  docx: default
format-links: [pdf, docx]

embed-resources: true puts the HTML in one file. format-links chooses which other formats the HTML page offers for download. format-links: false offers none. Give HTML and reveal.js different output-file names, or the second render overwrites the first.

Use a tool, or write one

Nathalie Lehmann and Quentin Blampey, Good practices (2024). Two kinds of bioinformatics project: start from tools that already exist, or write a new tool.

The second kind is the one that needs the practices below. Either kind ends in a result you have to be able to rerun.

final_final is not a history

Their picture of a project without Git: my_script.R, then my_script2.R because the first broke, then my_script_version_martin.R because a package and a function changed, then my_script_final.R, then my_script_final_final.R because everything broke.

The name records a mood. It does not record which lines changed.

Git answers the revision questions

The questions on their next slide:

which file made the figure in the article

what was deleted in the last version

which package versions the code used

why a small update for a revision changed the results

A commit is the answer to those four. A filename is not.

The hub is the shared copy

Their third Git slide is the local history plus a source-code hub. The hub is where a second person, or you on another machine, gets the same commits.

This deck’s hub is the GitHub repository named in the footer. The history of a slide is the history of the .qmd, not a second exported HTML.

Names, inputs, and the output

Lehmann and Blampey, on a function nobody else can read. Three obligations:

a function name and argument names that say what they are

the input types, and the values that are allowed

a precise description of what comes back

data and x fail the first test. So does a function whose only documentation is its body.

Put the documentation on a site

If you can, write the documentation as a site. Their example is gustaveroussy.github.io/sopa, a GitHub Pages site for the package.

For an R package, pkgdown builds that site from the roxygen comments and the vignettes. GitHub Pages, or the gh-pages branch this deck already uses, is a place to put it.

Dependencies move after you publish

Their timeline. You publish my_package 1.0.0 against one column of numpy, pandas, and PyTorch versions. Later columns are later releases of those libraries. Some of the earlier versions are already deprecated.

The package did not change. The ground under it did. A rerun on “whatever is current” is a different computation.

An unkept package goes stale

Their sentence: if you do not maintain the code, it becomes deprecated.

Maintenance here is a decision about each new column of dependency versions. Either you test my_package 1.0.0 against them, or you say which column it still supports.

Keep the old name until a stated date

A new version that renames annotate_cell_types() to get_cell_types() breaks every caller. They name Seurat as a package where that kind of change hurt.

Their first remedy keeps the old name, warns, and forwards. The warning in the slide says the function is deprecated and will be removed in January 2025.

def annotate_cell_types(X, d):
    warning("This function is deprecated and will be removed in Jan. 2025")
    return get_cell_types(X, markers=d)

Release notes name the version

Their second remedy is a release note tied to a version and a date. The example is v0.2.2, marked [0.2.2] - 2024-08-07, sorted into major, minor, and fixed.

A major line on that note says the new disk format is backward compatible. A minor line adds an argument. A fixed line names a bug. The version number is the contract. The note is what the contract changed.

Record the column you tested

The version number is not enough if the dependency column is unnamed. Write down the versions that belonged to that release: the numpy, pandas, and PyTorch numbers in their sketch, or the lockfile in an R project.

The Posit::conf 2024 dev-ops materials keep that column in renv.lock. A later rerun uses the lockfile, not today’s default install.

A test is code that checks a use

Lehmann and Blampey: testing means writing code that checks whether the package still does what it did.

A passing run is evidence about the cases you wrote down. It is the moment you can say the code is stable for those cases. A case you did not write is still unchecked.

The matrix shows which change broke

Their grid has package versions across the top and use cases down the side. A tick becomes a cross in one cell. The question the grid is for: which change broke what, when, on which operating system, and for which use.

That grid, run on every change, is continuous integration.

Actions runs the tests for you

The file that runs the grid is a workflow YAML. Their example is a run of the sopa package: the Actions log.

You open the log to see the failing case. You do not rerun the matrix by hand on a laptop and then forget which laptop.

A push to main can also publish

Continuous delivery is the same kind of file, with a deploy step at the end. This deck’s .github/workflows/publish.yml runs on a push to main: it checks out the repository, installs Quarto and R, renders, and publishes to gh-pages.

The getting-to-production workflow is the container version of that pattern. On a push to main it builds an image and pushes the image. The trigger is the same. The last step is a deploy instead of a test report.

Say which layer you are shipping

Before that deploy, the same session asks you to separate three layers.

The presentation layer is what a person clicks.

The processing layer is the work those clicks cause.

The data layer is where the values are stored and read.

A data update then changes the data layer. It does not require a new copy of the report, which is the Quarto path from the first slides.

The short list to take back

Lehmann and Blampey, the practices around the ones above: an assert that refuses a bad input, a style guide, a formatter and a linter, and a pre-commit hook so the check runs before the commit.

A package you want other people to use also needs a dependency manager, tests on more than one operating system and more than one language version, a documentation site, a release on CRAN, Bioconductor, or PyPI, and a version number you keep maintaining.

Before the quiz ❓

Six questions. Pick an option, then press x to check. Press q to reset that question.

Use the notation from the first hour: \(Y\) is the response, \(x\) is the covariate, \(\mu = \mathbb{E}(Y \mid x)\).

About eight minutes. Ask the room before anyone checks. The check key is x, so it does not steal the notes canvas.

A flux ratio of 0.97 (0.95 to 0.99), with \(p < 0.05\)

  • A large effect, because the test rejects.
  • Detected, and small. Ask whether 3% matters.
  • Too uncertain to quote, because the interval is narrow.

Sequencing failed for every core with pH below 4

pH is recorded for every core, including the cores with no sequences.

  • Missing completely at random: drop the cores.
  • Missing at random given pH. pH has to enter the model.
  • Missing not at random, so no model can use these rows.

Analyse SPRUCE warming as low versus high, split at \(+4.5^\circ\mathrm{C}\)

  • Reasonable: two groups are easier to explain.
  • Keep the regression across the five levels.
  • Either, if the \(p\)-value agrees.

Colony counts spread far above the fitted mean

The log-link slope is the quantity you care about.

  • Take \(\log(Y+1)\) and fit a Gaussian linear model.
  • Keep the log link. Replace the Poisson variance.
  • Trust the Poisson interval: the mean model is right.

A collar in each enclosure, measured every week

  • Each week is a new replicate of warming.
  • Ten enclosures. Weeks are repeats inside \(j\).
  • The replicate is the collar, because that is the sensor.

\(\exp(\hat\beta_1) = 4\) in a logit model of detection

One-unit increase in centred pH, other covariates held fixed.

  • The detection probability is multiplied by 4.
  • The odds of detection are multiplied by 4.
  • The log-odds increase by 4.

SAMPL, in workshop language 📝

Lang and Altman, SAMPL ask for the analysis to be explicit enough to audit. For this room, four lines are the minimum:

  1. Purpose: describe, compare, explain, predict, explore, or confirm
  2. Estimand, with the unit and the contrast
  3. Family, link, and what you did about missing rows
  4. The estimate, the interval, and the assumption you checked

“We used a mixed model” is not one of the four lines. The pitfalls card is write so the analysis can be rerun.

Rewrite the model you arrived with 📝

Take the analysis you named at the start. Replace one paragraph so that a colleague could say what \(\mu\) is.

  • What is \(Y\), and what is \(j\)?
  • Which of the twelve pitfalls is the one you are currently violating?
  • Does the family match the support of \(Y\)?
  • What would you refit tomorrow morning as a sensitivity analysis?

Ten minutes if the clock allows, otherwise five, and one example aloud. This is the second protected exercise.

A checklist you can paste above a script

  1. Question and purpose, in one sentence
  2. Estimand, including the population and the unit
  3. Experimental or observational, and where the treatment was applied
  4. Support of \(Y\): continuous, count, binary, composition, censored
  5. Dependence: site, enclosure, date, space
  6. Missingness: how much, and a mechanism you are willing to defend
  7. Linearity of the linear predictor, checked, not assumed
  8. Family check: a QQ plot of the right residual
  9. Sensitivity: one alternative that could move the claim
  10. A report that separates association from an assigned effect
